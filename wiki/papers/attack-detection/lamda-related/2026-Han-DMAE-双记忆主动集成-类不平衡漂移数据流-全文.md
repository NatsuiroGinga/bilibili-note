# DMAE: dual‑memory active ensemble learning for multi‑class imbalanced concept‑drifting data streams

Meng Han<sup>1</sup> · Yajie Xue<sup>1</sup> · Yikai Li<sup>1</sup> · Cuicui Ma<sup>1</sup> · Jian Ding<sup>1</sup> · Juan Li<sup>1</sup>

Received: 30 April 2026 / Accepted: 21 July 2026 © The Author(s) 2026

## Abstract

Multiclass imbalance and concept drift often coexist in real-world data streams, and the challenge becomes more severe under limited labeling budgets, where existing online ensemble and active learning methods still struggle to preserve minority-class recognition while adapting to evolving concepts. To address this issue, we propose DMAE, a dual-memory active ensemble learning method for multiclass imbalanced concept-drifting data streams. DMAE integrates an ensemble classifier, a drift detector, an instance sliding window, a label sliding window, a long-term prototype memory, and an initialization training sequence into a unified online framework for prediction, querying, memory maintenance, and model updating. To improve label eficiency, we develop a variable-threshold uncertainty strategy based on a decomposable asymmetric margin-threshold matrix, which combines a global threshold with class-pair-level corrections to focus queries on genuinely ambiguous, minority-relevant regions while controlling annotation cost. We further design drift-strength-aware dual-memory initialization (SDMI) and a probationary soft-replacement strategy (PDSR), which adaptively balance recent information and long-term prototypes according to online-estimated drift strength, supporting both historical-pattern retention and rapid adaptation under abrupt, gradual, and mixed drifts. We also propose a composite sample-weighting formulation, PCN-Weight, which jointly models boundary dificulty, class-imbalance status, sample–prototype relations, and temporal decay to guide incremental training and strengthen minority-class and hard-region representations. Experiments on 15 synthetic data streams and 5 real-world imbalanced data streams show that DMAE achieves more stable overall performance than ten state-of-the-art ensemble baselines in Accuracy, Kappa, G-Mean, and Recall, while remaining robust under diferent labeling rates and key hyperparameter settings.

## 1 Introduction

Multiclass imbalanced data streams often evolve over time, so the learner must cope with concept drift while maintaining reliable recognition of minority classes. In such nonstationary environments, drift may progressively invalidate previously learned decision boundaries and also reduce the efectiveness of active querying, because the most informative samples, class proportions, and confusion patterns all change over time. The problem becomes even more challenging under limited labeling budgets, where only a small fraction of arriving instances can be annotated and the resulting supervision is easily biased toward majority classes or currently ambiguous regions. Therefore, an efective online learner should jointly address drift adaptation, multiclass imbalance, and budget-aware label acquisition within a unified framework.

The joint presence of concept drift and multiclass imbal ance can substantially undermine the generalization abil ity of stream classifiers, and their interaction often leads to more severe and persistent performance degradation than either factor alone. For example, in imbalanced data-stream scenarios, many concept drift detectors tend to over-rely on the overall error rate, being more sensitive to changes in the majority class while responding sluggishly to abrupt distributional shifts within minority classes, thereby yielding drift-detection results biased toward the majority class (Korycki and Krawczyk 2021). Similarly, traditional imbal ance-handling strategies based on oversampling or undersampling often fail in drift scenarios with variable imbalance ratios, because such methods typically rely on static training assumptions and struggle to track in a timely manner the coevolution of class priors and class-conditional distributions. Although prior studies have explored imbalance mitigation, drift adaptation, online ensemble learning, and active querying from diferent perspectives, most of them address only part of the target problem and still lack a unified treatment of multiclass imbalance, mixed concept drift, constrained labe ling budgets, and stable online ensemble evolution. In par ticular, the coupling among query bias, imbalance estima tion, drift response, and minority-class preservation remain insuficiently addressed in current approaches. Moreover, most existing studies implicitly assume that true labels can be continuously and suficiently obtained during training, an assumption that often does not hold in real-world scenario with high labeling costs and limited resources. When the labeling budget is constrained, the superposition of multiclass imbalance and concept drift makes online active learn ing even more challenging, because the model can obtain supervision only from a small number of selected queried samples, while a large proportion of arriving instances remain unlabeled, thereby further exacerbating the dificulty of imbalance estimation and drift adaptation. To the best of our knowledge, an eficient unified method that simulta neously addresses these challenges under limited labeling conditions is still lacking.

To address the above challenges, this paper proposes a dual-memory active ensemble learning method, DMAE, for multiclass imbalanced concept-drifting data streams, aiming to simultaneously enhance minority-class recognition capability, drift adaptability, and overall stability under limited labeling budgets. The overall framework consists of an online ensemble classifier composed of multiple base classifiers, an ADWIN-based drift detection module, a label sliding window for approximately unbiased estimation of the current class distribution, class-wise short-term sample windows and long-term prototype memory, and an initialization training sample sequence used to reconstruct new base classifiers when drift occurs. On this basis, we propose an uncertainty modeling and hybrid labeling mechanism based on decomposable asymmetric margins, through the collaboration of random labeling and BAM<sup>2</sup>-Lite-driven active labeling: on the one hand, the random subprocess provides approximately unbiased multiclass labeled samples for the label sliding window; on the other hand, the active subprocess adaptively adjusts the margin threshold at the class-pair scale, concentrating the limited labeling budget on genuinely ambiguous boundary regions relevant to minority classes. Subsequently, we design a drift-strength-aware dual-memory initialization and soft replacement mechanism, SDMI-PDSR, which simultaneously maintains a short-term sample window and a long-term prototype memory for each class, and uses the drift-strength index provided by ADWIN to adaptively control the mixing ratio of the two in the initialization training sequence. It then determines whether to retain or discard candidate base classifiers through performance-and-diversity utility evaluation within a limited tria period, thereby balancing historical pattern inheritance and rapid adaptation to new concepts under abrupt, gradual, and hybrid drift. Finally, we propose a prototype-contrast and class-quota-normalized comprehensive sample-weighting formulation, PCN-Weight, which unifies decision-boundary dificulty, the dynamically imbalanced state estimated by the label sliding window, the geometric relationship between samples and intra-class prototypes, and time decay into a single training weight. In this way, the allocation of training intensity during online bagging updates is regulated more finely along both the temporal axis and the class axis, thereby improving the utilization eficiency of limited labeled samples and strengthening the representation quality of minority classes and locally dificult regions.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:

(1) A dual-memory active ensemble framework termed DMAE is proposed for multiclass imbalanced concept-drifting data streams. It systematically integrates an online ensemble classifier, a drift-detection module, a label sliding window for approximately unbiased estimation of the current class distribution, and classwise short-term sample windows and long-term prototype memory into a unified online architecture. The resulting framework enables collaborative modeling of prediction, label querying, memory maintenance, and model-structure updating under the joint presence of fixed and variable multiclass imbalance ratios, virtual and real drift together with their hybrid forms, limited labeling budgets, and adaptive ensemble size.

(2) A decomposable asymmetric margin-threshold matrix and a corridor-gated threshold field $( \mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { - L i t e } )$ are developed to model class-pair asymmetry at a fine granularity. They jointly capture class potential, historical confusion patterns, and evolving imbalance structure to derive direction-aware threshold biases between class pairs. The adjustment is activated only within a predefined uncertainty corridor, while a conservative threshold cap prevents the efective query threshold from exceeding the global baseline. In this way, limited labeling budgets are concentrated on genuinely ambiguous and minority-relevant boundary instances without sacrificing the original safety margin.

(3) A drift-strength-aware dual-memory initialization scheme (SDMI) and a performance-and-diversitydriven soft replacement strategy (PDSR) are proposed to support robust ensemble evolution under concept drift. For each class, DMAE maintains a short-term sample window and a long-term prototype memory, and uses an online drift-strength estimate to adaptively set the mixing ratio when constructing the initialization sample sequence. A newly created base classifier is trained from this sequence and admitted as a trial member; it is retained only when its trial-period utility indicates improvement over at least one existing member. This mechanism mitigates cold-start efects, reduces excessive forgetting, and avoids abrupt ensemble resets across abrupt, gradual, and hybrid drift regimes.

(4) A prototype-contrast and class-quota-normalized comprehensive sample-weighting formulation (PCN-Weight) is constructed. It aggregates margin-based prediction dificulty, the imbalance degree estimated from the approximately unbiased label sliding window, the relative proximity between a sample and its intraclass prototypes, and a time-decay factor into a single scalar weight. The resulting weight emphasizes hard and minority-relevant samples locally, while imposing a soft quota constraint on the cumulative efective contribution of majority classes globally, thereby regulating incremental training intensity along both temporal and class dimensions.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on active learning for data streams, imbalanced data-stream classification, concept drift adaptation and ensemble learning, and integrated methods under multiple challenges. Section 3 details the proposed DMAE framework and its key components, including the hybrid uncertainty-based querying strategy, the drift-strength-aware dual-memory initialization with soft replacement, and the PCN-Weight reweighting mechanism. Section 4 presents experimental settings, baseline methods, and evaluation protocols, and systematically verifies the efectiveness and robustness of DMAE on diverse synthetic and real-world data streams. Section 5 concludes the paper and outlines directions for future work.

## 2 Related work

## 2.1 Active learning for data streams

In label-scarce streaming scenarios, active learning improves model performance by selectively requesting true labels for only a small number of instances, and is therefore a key avenue for controlling annotation costs. Common strategies can be broadly grouped into three categories: uncertainty-based strategies (Nguyen et al. 2022), which select instances according to predictive uncertainty or distance to the decision boundary; random-sampling strategies (Zhang et al. 2023), which draw instances more uniformly from the stream to preserve distributional representativeness; and hybrid strategies (Liu et al. 2021), which combine uncertainty and representativeness to better maintain accuracy and robustness under limited labeling budgets. Compared with traditional ofline supervised learning, however, active learning in data streams must additionally account for continuous distribution evolution and delayed or partial supervision, which makes strategy design substantially more challenging.

One representative line of work embeds label querying directly into existing supervised or ensemble models. Salama et al. proposed an ensemble active learning model for heterogeneous data analytics (Salama et  al. 2022), which actively selects a small number of high-value samples to reduce manual annotation cost and leverages ensemble learning to improve classification stability; however, it mainly targets static heterogeneous data and does not explicitly address continuous distribution evolution in streaming environments. Niu et al. proposed ADESSA (Niu et al. 2023), which combines margin sampling with democratic co-learning and balanced sampling to construct a limited-label training set from both manually labeled and automatically labeled high-confidence samples, but its efectiveness depends strongly on the reliability of pseudo-labels. Polyzos et al. proposed a weighted Gaussian-process ensemble framework (Polyzos et al. 2024), which improves sample-selection eficiency by adaptively weighting ensemble members and sampling criteria, yet it is still formulated for more general active learning settings rather than multiclass imbalanced drifting streams. Wang et al. proposed MOOB and MUOB (Wang et al. 2016), which improve performance on multiclass imbalanced data streams through resampling and online ensembles; nevertheless, if uncertainty-based querying is directly overlaid on top of such methods, the queried set may become systematically concentrated on hard-to-classify regions, thereby distorting the labeled subset used for distribution estimation. Park et al. proposed an active learning strategy for concept-drifting data streams (Park and Kang 2016), which incorporates drift-severity assessment into incremental classification and dynamically adjusts querying probabilities; despite its low computational overhead, its efectiveness depends on the quality of drift estimation and may degrade under noisy or complex drift patterns.

Another line of work jointly designs the querying mechanism and the streaming learning framework at the system level. Guo et al. proposed SSALE-EIS (Guo et al. 2024), which integrates semi-supervised exploitation of unlabeled data and active querying of key samples within an online ensemble framework, but still needs to balance pseudo-label stability and querying eficiency during rapid distribution evolution. Halder et al. proposed AACE-DI (Halder et al. 2023), which combines clustering-based ensemble learning with automatic uncertainty adjustment to reduce expert labeling efort in drifting imbalanced streams, but its performance depends on the stability of clustering structure under drift. Zhang et al. proposed ROALE-DI (Zhang et al. 2022), which combines a long-term stable classifier with dynamic classifiers and updates member weights through reinforcement signals to improve minority-class sensitivity under drift and imbalance; however, the method remain sensitive to reward design and the stability of online reinforcement updates. Li et al. proposed AdaAL-MID (Han et al. 2024), which co-designs ensemble classification, drift detection, and hybrid querying for multiclass imbalanced drifting streams, but under strict labeling budgets it still faces nontrivial coordination among query frequency, class coverage, and model-update pace. Krawczyk et al. proposed EAL-MAB (Krawczyk and Cano 2019), which formulates querying decisions and strategy switching as a multi-armed bandit problem, yet its gains depend heavily on the diversity and complementarity of the ensemble members.

Overall, active learning for data streams has made substantial progress under labeling-budget constraints, including uncertainty-based querying, hybrid querying, and integration with online ensembles. However, when multiclass imbalance and hybrid concept drift coexist over long horizons, three structural limitations remain: querying decisions are still often driven by a single uncertainty signal, the bias introduced by active querying is frequently entangled with downstream statistical estimation, and existing mechanisms often struggle to simultaneously maintain minorityclass protection, drift-response speed, and label-utilization eficiency.

## 2.2 Imbalanced data stream classification

Research on imbalanced data-stream classification initially focused mainly on binary-class settings (Salehi and Khedmati 2025; Zhai et al. 2022; Wang and wang 2023; Lu et al. 2017; Zhao et al. 2023), where the antagonistic relation between a single majority class and a single minority class is relatively easy to characterize. In multiclass settings, by contrast, whether a class behaves as a majority or a minority depends on its relative relation to other classes, and decomposing the task into multiple binary subtasks may lose important information about inter-class relativistic structure and transfer patterns (García et al. 2018). As a result, multiclass imbalance is substantially more difficult than binary imbalance, especially when minorityto-minority confusion and class-role switching must be considered simultaneously.

To improve minority-class learning in streaming environments, a large body of work has explored cost-sensitive learning, online resampling, and dynamically weighted ensembles. Wang et al. proposed OLI^2DS (You et al. 2023), which incrementally updates the classifier while integrating active querying and reweighting strategies to improve minority recognition; however, its performance can degrade when drift occurs frequently and the updating pace cannot keep up with rapid distributional changes. Du et al. proposed DyAbst (Krawczyk and Cano 2018), an online ensemble method that dynamically estimates misclassification costs, performs balanced sampling over incoming instances, and updates base-classifier weights, but its robustness depends strongly on the stability of cost estimation under rapid drift. Gao et al. proposed a dynamic selection ensemble framework (Jiao et al. 2024), which combines drift detection, sub set selection, and resampling to alleviate imbalance; yet the subset-evaluation process may incur substantial overhead in multiclass settings. Lu et al. proposed DWMIL (Lu et al. 2017), which adjusts voting weights according to classifier performance and age and later introduced an adaptive-window extension, but its weight adaptation may still lag when class roles reverse. Lin et al. proposed ESOS-ELM (Mirza et al. 2015), which exploits historical information through resampling and a storage module, but requires prior knowledge of the imbalance ratio and is therefore less suitable for streams with rapidly changing class proportions. Krawczyk et al. proposed EOBag (Du et al. 2021), in which low-confi dence learners abstain from voting and a dynamic threshold determines the participating member set; however, its efectiveness is sensitive to threshold adaptation and fluctuations in the efective voting pool. Jian et al. proposed OEC (Jian et al. 2024), which uses a normalized exponentiated-gradient scheme to combine multiple online classifiers and improve robustness under noisy labels, but its gains depend on the complementarity of the base learners and the alignment between weight adaptation and drift phases.

Overall, existing methods for imbalanced data-stream classification have improved minority-class learnability through reweighting, resampling, cost-sensitive learning, and online ensembles. Nevertheless, many of them are still designed under binary or fully supervised assumptions, and they do not explicitly provide a unified treatment of multiclass imbalance relations, evolving class-pair confusion structure, and limited-label online updating.

## 2.3 Concept drift adaptation and ensemble learning

In data streams where concept drift and imbalance coexist, concept drift may appear together with changing class priors and evolving class-conditional distributions. Existing solutions can be broadly categorized into block-based schemes and instance-wise online schemes. Although both categories contribute to drift adaptation, they exhibit diferent limitations when multiclass imbalance, hybrid drift, and limited labeling budgets are present simultaneously.

Block-based approaches usually partition the stream into consecutive chunks, perform rebalancing or cost adjustment within each block, and combine block-level drift detection with ensemble updating. The Recursive Ensemble Approach (REA) (Chen and He 2011) introduces a distance metric to select minority-class samples from historical blocks and supplements them into the current block, while dynamically fus ing historical hypotheses for prediction. ACDWM (Lu et al. 2020) dynamically adjusts block size and ensemble weights to balance stability and adaptivity under drift. HBBE (Mahdi et al. 2021) combines multiclass drift detection with a blockweighting mechanism to handle abrupt and gradual drift. WECOI (Czarnowski 2022) embeds imbalance handling into block construction and uses one-class decomposition together with oversampling and weighted ensemble replacement. WENNML (Wu et al. 2023), although designed for multi-label streams, also follows a sequential block-training strategy with weighted voting and interpretable updating. These methods provide important support for learning from streams where drift and imbalance coexist, but they remain largely driven by block-level statistics and block-level triggers. As a result, they are often insensitive to rapid withinblock changes, may lag in updating under abrupt drift, and still rely heavily on suficiently available labeled data within each block.

In contrast, instance-wise online methods adapt in real time through continual updating, allowing classifiers and ensemble weights to evolve together with the stream. This paradigm is better aligned with rapidly changing environments, but it also increases design complexity because drift adaptation, imbalance mitigation, and budgeted label acquisition may all need to be coordinated within the same online loop. ROALE-DI (Zhang et al. 2022), for example, integrates reinforcement-guided label selection and classifier updating to cope with drifting imbalanced streams under limited budgets, but is sensitive to reward design and policy stability. More broadly, many instance-wise approaches improve adaptability through continuous updating and dynamic selection, yet they still face challenges in computational eficiency, robust memory coordination, and stable adaptation under multiclass proportion shifts and accumulated noise.

Overall, block-based methods are efective when drift is relatively smooth and labels are suficiently available, whereas instance-wise methods are generally more responsive to evolving distributions. However, under rapid withinblock drift, multiclass imbalance, variable imbalance ratios, and strict labeling constraints, existing drift-adaptation and ensemble-learning mechanisms still struggle to jointly maintain minority-class protection, timely drift response, and stable model evolution.

## 2.4 Integrated methods under multiple challenges

In recent years, integrated studies on multiclass imbalanced data streams and concept drift have gradually increased, yet most existing methods still combine only a subset of the challenges involved. For instance, MOS-ELM (Mirza and Lin 2016) was among the earlier attempts to handle hybrid drift together with variable multiclass imbalance ratios, but its block-based processing paradigm cannot respond promptly to drift changes occurring within a single block. PEP (Hidalgo et al. 2021) adjusts ensemble diver sity strength online through parameter estimation, aiming to maintain more stable component diferences under drift; however, its focus remains largely on diversity regulation and does not explicitly characterize evolving multiclass imbalance ratios, minority-to-minority confusion patterns, or the risks associated with asymmetric costs. ROSE (Cano and Krawczyk 2022) uses online feature subsets, background ensembles, class-wise sliding windows, and self-adjusting bagging to improve robustness on multiclass imbalanced drifting streams, but it still mainly relies on window memory and resampling-based structural robustness, and lacks dedi cated closed-loop mechanisms for query bias under limited labeling budgets. Incremental decision-tree drift-mitigation methods (Tarazodar et al. 2024) improve drift discovery and structural adaptation through subtree monitoring and incremental updates, but they are closer to a single-mode continual-update paradigm and do not inherently provide ensemble-level diversity maintenance or stable fusion under multiclass imbalance. EDAC (Gong and Dong 2024) uses entropy diferences as cues to balance incoming blocks, combines density sampling to distinguish minority-sample quality, and adopts self-feedback strategies for initializing ensemble weights, yet it still operates at the block level and thus remains vulnerable to delayed response under rapid within-block drift and class-role switching.

Taken together, prior studies have made meaningful progress in active querying, imbalance handling, drift adaptation, and online ensemble updating, but they still fall short of jointly addressing four requirements of the target scenario considered in this work: direction-sensitive query control under multiclass imbalance, approximately unbiased estimation of evolving class distributions under active labeling, drift-aware memory reconstruction that balances recent and long-term information, and stable candidate-classifier admission and replacement within an online ensemble. These limitations motivate the DMAE framework proposed in this paper. From a broader methodological perspective, recent research has increasingly emphasized uncertaintyaware and reliability-driven learning under incomplete, heterogeneous, or dynamically evolving observations. For example, uncertainty quantification has been explored to improve the reliability of model inference under distributed and heterogeneous conditions (Chen et al. 2025). Acquisition-function design has also been studied as a principled way to balance information gain and decision cost in sequential query processes (Chen et al. 2026a). In addition, temporal adaptive modeling has been investigated for sparse and uncertain event sequences under evolving environments (Chen et al. 2026b). These studies provide useful conceptual references for understanding online learning as a process of uncertainty estimation, adaptive information acquisition, and reliability-oriented updating. Nevertheless, they are not specifically designed for multiclass imbalanced data streams with concept drift and limited labels. Compared with these broader uncertainty-aware learning paradigms, DMAE focuses on the streaming classification setting and integrates query-source separation, class-pair-level uncertainty control, drift-aware memory reconstruction, and weighted online ensemble updating into a unified active learning framework.

Together, these components enable unified coordination of label querying, memory maintenance, drift response, and incremental ensemble updating under limited labeling budgets. ## 2.6 Overall framework and workflow

This subsection presents the overall framework and online workflow of DMAE. DMAE integrates four tightly coupled components, namely hybrid uncertainty-based querying via B $\mathbf { \delta A M } ^ { 2 } -$ Lite, drift-strength-aware dual-memory initialization via SDMI, utility-driven soft replacement via PDSR, and weighted online updating via PCN-Weight. These components are organized within a unified online loop to coordinate prediction, budgeted label querying, class-distribution estimation, memory maintenance, drift response, and ensemble evolution. In this way, DMAE does not treat active querying, imbalance estimation, drift adaptation, and ensemble replacement as isolated operations, but connects them through a continuous online learning process.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the framework mainly consists of an online ensemble classifier E composed of D base classifiers, an ADWIN-based drift detection module, a label sliding window L for approximately unbiased estimation of the current class distribution, class-wise short-term sample windows $R _ { c }$ , long-term prototype memories $H _ { c } ,$ , and an initialization training sequence I used to reconstruct a candidate classifier after drift detection. For each incoming instance, the current ensemble first produces posterior probabilities and a margin-based confidence estimate. Under the global labeling budget, the hybrid querying module then decides whether to request the true label through random querying or $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { - } \mathbf { I }$ ite -guided active querying. Once a label is acquired, the sample is used for PCN-Weight-guided online updating and class-wise memory maintenance. Meanwhile, ADWIN monitors online prediction errors under the available queried labels and estimates the drift strength. When significant drift is detected, DMAE invokes SDMI to reconstruct a candidate classifier from the dual-memory structure, and PDSR then determines whether the candidate should replace an existing ensemble member after a trial period.

Let the data stream be denoted as ${ \cal S } = \{ x _ { 1 } , x _ { 2 } , \dots , x _ { t } , \dots \}$ where $x _ { t }$ is the incoming instance at time t , and its true label $y _ { t }$ is unavailable unless the algorithm explicitly requests it from an oracle. For each incoming instance, the ensemble classifier E outputs a posterior probability vector $\mathrm { p } _ { t } .$ , the predicted label $\widehat { y } _ { t } ,$ , and a margin score $m _ { t }$ . The posterior vector and margin are formally defined in Section 3.2. A smaller margin indicates that the ensemble has lower confidence in distinguishing the two most probable classes, and therefore the margin is used as the basic uncertainty signal for subsequent BAM<sup>2</sup>-Lite -based active querying.

Under the global labeling budget, DMAE adopts a hybrid querying strategy that combines random querying and $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } { \cdot } \mathbf { L i t e }$ -guided active querying. Let b denote the labeling-budget ratio, t denote the number of processed instances, and l denote the number of labels already queried from the oracle. At time t, the maximum number of labels allowed by the cumulative budget is $B _ { t } = \lfloor b t \rfloor$ . A new label can be requested only when $l < B _ { t }$ ⌊ ⌋. Thus, random querying, $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { - } ]$ Lite uncertainty querying, selective sampling, and drift-stage exploration all share the same global labeling budget. If the remaining budget is exhausted, DMAE does not request the label of the current instance and pushes a Null placeholder into the label sliding window. SDMI and PDSR do not consume additional labels; they only reuse the labels already obtained under the same budget.

To clarify the label-querying workflow, DMAE distinguishes three cases for each incoming instance. First, if the random-query branch is triggered and the global budget is still available, the oracle label $y _ { t }$ is requested. This label is inserted into the label sliding window $L ,$ and the labeled sample $( x _ { t } , y _ { t } )$ is used for model updating, memory maintenance, and drift monitoring. Second, if the instance is selected by the $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \cdot \mathbf { I }$ Lite active-query branch, the oracle label $y _ { t }$ is also requested and used for ensemble updating, short-term sample-window updating, long-term prototypememory updating, $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { - } \mathbf { I }$ Lite parameter adaptation, and PDSR utility evaluation. However, because actively queried instances are concentrated around uncertain decision boundaries, directly using them for class-distribution estimation may introduce query-selection bias. Therefore, the corresponding position in the label sliding window L is filled with Null rather than $y _ { t } .$ . Third, if the instance is not queried, Null is pushed into $L ,$ and the instance does not participate in supervised model updating.

Accordingly, the label sliding window L is used mainly for approximately unbiased estimation of the current class distribution based on random-query labels. Let $n _ { c } ( t )$ denote the number of non-null labels of class c in the current label window. The estimated class prior can be computed as $\widehat { \pi } _ { c } ( t ) = n _ { c } ( t ) / ( \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { C } n _ { j } ( t ) + \epsilon _ { 0 } )$ , where $\epsilon _ { 0 }$ is a small smoothing constant. Based on this estimated class distribution, DMAE obtains the current imbalance state of each class and provides class-distribution information for $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \cdot \mathbf { I }$ Lite and PCN-Weight. This design separates two diferent uses of queried labels: random-query labels provide a relatively unbiased statistical basis for class-distribution estimation, whereas active-query labels improve learning around informative and minority-relevant boundary regions.

Drift detection is also performed under the partial-label setting. Since unqueried instances have no ground-truth labels, ADWIN can only be updated when a queried label is available. To reduce the bias caused by boundary-focused active querying, the prediction errors of random-query labels are used as the main error stream for ADWIN-based drift monitoring. For a random-query sample, the supervised error is computed as $e _ { t } = \mathbb { I } ( \widehat { y } _ { t } \neq y _ { t } )$ . Active-query labels are still used for model updating, memory updating, $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { - } ]$ Lite adaptation, and PDSR utility evaluation. Their prediction errors may also be recorded as auxiliary evidence for estimating drift severity, but they are not used alone as the primary source for class-distribution estimation. This treatment avoids confusing the high uncertainty of actively queried boundary samples with true distributional drift.

Based on the above protocol, the online workflow of DMAE can be summarized as follows. For an incoming unlabeled instance $x _ { t } .$ , the ensemble classifier first outputs $\mathsf { p } _ { t } , \widehat { y } _ { t }$ , and $m _ { t }$ . The global budget condition $l < B _ { t }$ is then checked. If the budget is available, the random-query branch may be triggered with probability $\varepsilon .$ Otherwise, the $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \cdot \mathbf { I }$ Lite -based uncertainty strategy is invoked to determine whether the current instance should be queried according to the class-pair-level threshold and the margin $m _ { t }$ . If no query is made, Null is inserted into L, and the algorithm proceeds to the next instance.

When a label is requested, the sample is routed according to its query source. A random-query label is inserted into L and used for class-distribution estimation. An active-query label is used for training and memory updating, while its position in L is filled with Null to avoid contaminating the class-prior estimation. The non-null entries in L are then used to estimate the current class distribution and imbalance indicators. For each queried sample $( x _ { t } , y _ { t } )$ , DMAE computes its prediction dificulty, class-imbalance state, prototype-relative position, and temporal information, and obtains the comprehensive training weight through PCN-Weight. The weighted sample is used to update the ensemble classifier by online bagging. Meanwhile, it is stored in the corresponding short-term sample window $R _ { y _ { t } }$ , and the long-term prototype memory $H _ { y _ { t } }$ is updated according to the representativeness and diversity criteria. In this way, shortterm memory captures recent local distributional information, whereas long-term memory preserves relatively stable representative patterns.

When ADWIN detects significant drift, DMAE computes the drift-strength indicator $s _ { \mathrm { d r i f t } }$ and invokes SDMI to construct an initialization sequence I from the short-term windows and long-term prototype memories. The mixing ratio between short-term and long-term memory is adaptively determined according to $s _ { \mathrm { d r i f t } }$ . A new candidate classifier $C _ { \mathrm { n e w } }$ is then trained on I with time-decayed PCN weights. After SDMI initialization, $C _ { \mathrm { n e w } }$ does not immediately replace an existing ensemble member. Instead, PDSR temporarily inserts it as a trial member and evaluates its utility during a limited trial period. The utility score jointly considers overall accuracy, minority-oriented performance, and prediction diversity. If $C _ { \mathrm { n e w } }$ achieves better utility than at least one existing member, it replaces the member with the lowest utility; otherwise, it is discarded and the original ensemble structure remains unchanged. Through this trial-based mechanism, DMAE avoids abrupt replacement caused by short-term fluctuations or drift misdetection.

## 2.7  Hybrid uncertainty and querying strategy based on BAM<sup>2</sup>‑lite

This subsection presents the hybrid uncertainty and querying mechanism of DMAE for multiclass imbalanced data streams. Centered on a decomposable asymmetric marginthreshold structure and its corridor-gated $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { - } \mathbf { l }$ Lite strategy, the proposed mechanism unifies random querying, structured uncertainty assessment, and selective sampling within a single framework. The random-query branch provides the label sliding window with an approximately unbiased estimate of the class distribution, whereas $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \cdot \mathbf { I }$ Lite focuses on selecting informative boundary samples for model updating and memory maintenance. The decomposable asymmetric margin-threshold structure characterizes, at the class-pair scale, the separability among diferent classes and the directional querying preference. $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { - } ]$ Lite further introduces class potential, pairwise interaction, drift intensity, and imbalance-related information to parameterize and smoothly control these pairwise thresholds, thereby prioritizing the acquisition of valuable boundary samples under a strict labeling budget.

Let the ensemble classifier be $E ,$ , and denote the predicted posterior probability of an incoming stream instance x as $p _ { E } ( c \mid x )$ . Then $y _ { c _ { 1 } }$ is defined as in Eq. (1).

$$
y _ {c _ {1}} = \arg \max _ {c} p _ {E} (c \mid x), y _ {c _ {2}} = \arg \max _ {c \neq y _ {c _ {1}}} p _ {E} (c \mid x)\tag{1}
$$

The margin is defined as in Eq. (2).

$$
\operatorname{margin} (x) = p _ {E} \left(y _ {c _ {1}} \mid x\right) - p _ {E} \left(y _ {c _ {2}} \mid x\right)\tag{2}
$$

To explicitly distinguish directional heterogeneity among classes during active querying, DMAE maintains class-pairlevel threshold information for each ordered class pair $( i , j )$ Unlike a fixed global threshold, the class-pair threshold is adjusted according to the evolving class distribution and the historical confusion pattern between classes. In the proposed implementation, the label sliding window L provides an online estimate of the current imbalance state based on its non-null random-query labels. Since $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \cdot$ Lite is used before the true label of the current active-query sample is obtained, Algorithm 2 does not directly update the label window or the class-pair state. Instead, it only determines whether the current sample should be actively queried and whether it lies in the uncertainty corridor. After Algorithm 1 obtains the true label of a queried corridor sample, the $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { - } \mathbf { I }$ Lite state variables are updated through UpdateBAM<sup>2</sup>ParamsLite. This design separates active-query decision making from label acquisition and label-window maintenance. The potential vector 𝛼 records class-level learning dificulty or querying tendency, the interaction matrix G records directional confusion between class pairs, and the imbalance-related term reflects the current classdistribution bias estimated from L. Together, these components gradually adjust the initially uniform threshold behaviour into a direction-sensitive class-pair threshold field, while keeping the global label-routing rule consistent with Algorithm 1.

Based on the above class-pair threshold information, the basic uncertainty strategy can be described as follows: if margin(x) $\leq M _ { \mathrm { e f f } } .$ , the ensemble is considered unable to confidently distinguish $y _ { c _ { 1 } }$ from $y _ { c _ { 2 } }$ , and the current instance is selected for active querying. Otherwise, the algorithm enters the selective sampling stage and decides whether to further query this sample by considering the remaining labeling budget and the redundancy margin margin(x) $- M _ { \mathrm { e f f } } .$ . This design ensures suficient attention to hard samples near the decision boundary and avoids over-consuming the limited budget on highly confident samples. To further improve adaptability and interpretability, $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { - } \mathbf { l }$ Lite constructs a decomposable asymmetric margin-threshold component for each ordered class pair $( i , j )$ , as shown in Eq. (3).

$$
\widetilde {M} _ {i j} = \theta \sigma (\alpha_ {i} - \alpha_ {j}) + \lambda_ {G} G _ {i j} + \mu_ {d r i f t} s _ {d r i f t} + \nu_ {i m b} \phi_ {i j}\tag{3}
$$

In Eq. (3), each term has a specific practical meaning. The potential parameter $\alpha _ { i }$ describes the class-level querying ten dency of class i. A larger diference $\alpha _ { i } - \alpha _ { j }$ indicates that the ordered class pair $( i , j )$ requires a diferent query sensitivity from its reverse direction $( j , i )$ . Therefore, the term $\tau ( \alpha _ { i } - \alpha _ { j } )$ introduces directional asymmetry into the threshold component. The interaction term $G _ { i j }$ records the historical confusion tendency from class i to class j. If samples predicted as class i are frequently confused with class $j , G _ { i j }$ increases the influence of this class pair in the active-query decision. The drift term $s _ { \mathrm { d r i f t } }$ represents the current concept-drift intensity estimated from the ADWIN-based error changes. When the stream becomes unstable, this term increases the sensitivity of $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \cdot$ Lite to boundary samples in the drift period. The imbalance-related term $\phi _ { i j }$ reflects the current class-pair imbalance structure estimated from the non-null entries in the label sliding window L. It can be constructed from the relative imbalance indicators of classes i and $j ,$ for example through the conditional frequency ratio log $\frac { 1 + \mathrm { { i m b } } _ { i } } { 1 + \mathrm { { i m b } } _ { j } }$ . The coefficients $\lambda _ { G } , \mu _ { \mathrm { d r i f t } }$ , and $\nu _ { \mathrm { i m b } }$ control the contributions of historical confusion, drift intensity, and imbalance bias, respectively. Therefore, $\widetilde { M } _ { i j }$ is not a single heuristic threshold, but a decomposable class-pair component that combines four interpretable factors: class-level potential diference, pairwise confusion, drift intensity, and imbalance structure. This design allows $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } .$ -Lite to distinguish diferent ordered class pairs rather than applying the same threshold to all class boundaries. It also explains why the threshold structure is asymmetric: the query preference from class i to class j may be diferent from that from class j to class i, especially when one class is underrepresented or when the two direc tions show diferent historical confusion patterns. In this way, the decomposable structure provides an interpretable basis for adaptive class-pair-level uncertainty querying.

On top of the decomposable structure, we introduce $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } -$ Lite to smoothly control the threshold matrix and enable it progressively. First, we linearly interpolate the recommended threshold $\widetilde { M } _ { i j }$ with the global baseline threshold 𝜃 using a dynamic mixing coeficient $\beta _ { e f f } \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ yielding the $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \cdot \mathbf { I }$ Lite class-pair threshold as in Eq. (4).

$$
M _ {i j} ^ {B A M 2} = (1 - \beta_ {e f f}) \theta + \beta_ {e f f} \widetilde {M} _ {i j}\tag{4}
$$

The mixing coefficient $\beta _ { e f f }$ is adaptively adjusted according to the consumed labeling-budget ratio, the current drift intensity, and the predictive uncertainty of the sample. The final $\beta _ { e f f }$ is defined as in Eq. (5).

$$
\beta_ {e f f} = \mathrm{clip} _ {[ 0, 1 ]} (\beta_ {m i n} + (\beta_ {m a x} - \beta_ {m i n}) r _ {l a b e l} + \kappa_ {1} s _ {d r i f t} + \kappa_ {2} (1 - p _ {m a x}))\tag{5}
$$

where $r _ { l a b e l }$ is the ratio of consumed labeling budget, $p _ { m a x } = \operatorname* { m a x } _ { c } p _ { E } ( c \mid x )$ is the maximum posterior probability of the current sample, and $\beta _ { m i n } , \beta _ { m a x } , \kappa _ { 1 } , \kappa _ { 2 }$ are hyperparameters; $\mathrm { c l i p } _ { [ 0 , 1 ] } ( \cdot )$ truncates the result into [0,1]. Thus, when labeling history is limited, drift is not evident, or the prediction is highly confident, $\beta _ { e f f }$ stays close to $\beta _ { m i n }$ and the model mainly follows the original threshold; as the budget becomes more available, drift intensifies, and predictions become more uncertain, $\beta _ { e f f }$ increases smoothly, and the structured information (class potential, confusion, imbalance, etc.) gradually exerts stronger influence on class-pair thresholds.

To maintain overall stability while introducing structured thresholds, $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } -$ Lite further adopts a corridor mechanism together with a conservative threshold cap. Let the margin of the current sample be margin(x). When its distance to the baseline threshold 𝜃 satisfies $\mid \operatorname* { m a r g i n } ( x ) - \theta \mid \leq \delta$ , the sample is regarded as falling inside the uncertainty corridor. Only in this case does $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \cdot$ -Lite activate the decomposable class-pair threshold adjustment. Outside the corridor, the strategy falls back to the baseline threshold, so as to avoid unnecessary structured perturbation in high-confidence regions. Inside the corridor, the final uncertainty threshold used for querying is defined as in Eq. (6).

$$
M _ {e f f} (y _ {c _ {1}}, y _ {c _ {2}}) = \min (\theta , M _ {y _ {c _ {1}} y _ {c _ {2}}} ^ {B A M 2})\tag{6}
$$

Since the query decision is triggered when mar $\mathrm { g i n } ( x ) \leq M _ { \mathrm { e f f } } ( y _ { c _ { 1 } } , y _ { c _ { 2 } } )$ , Eq.  (6) ensures that the adaptive threshold does not exceed the global baseline threshold 𝜃. Therefore, $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 }$ -Lite does not arbitrarily enlarge the query region beyond the baseline uncertainty range. This conservative cap is important under a strict labelling budget because it prevents the structured class-pair adjustment from over-consuming labels, while still allowing the model to refine the query decision for samples located in the sensitive threshold corridor.

In summary, the proposed hybrid uncertainty and querying strategy is jointly driven by three layers of mechanisms. First, random querying provides an approximately unbiased statistical foundation for estimating class imbalance. Second, the decomposable asymmetric marginthreshold structure explicitly characterizes directionsensitive querying preference at the class-pair scale by encoding class potential, confusion patterns, drift intensity, and imbalance structure into the threshold space. Third, $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \mathbf { - } ]$ Lite performs fine-grained threshold refinement for key boundary samples inside the uncertainty corridor through dynamic mixing and the conservative threshold cap. Their combination enables the algorithm, under strict labelling-budget constraints, to prioritize informative boundary samples around minority classes and drift intervals, while avoiding excessive label consumption in high-confidence regions.

For instances whose margin is slightly larger than $M _ { \mathrm { e f f } } .$ relying only on the strict uncertainty condition may be overly conservative. Therefore, Algorithm 2 further considers the remaining budget and the redundancy distance from the threshold. It first computes the remaining sampling budget as sampbudget = b − costNow, and the margin redundancy as $q = \mathrm { m a r g i n } ( x ) - M _ { \mathrm { e f f } }$ . When sampbudget > 0, the supplementary sampling probability is adaptively computed by sampbudget $\begin{array} { r } { \overleftarrow { } \frac { \mathrm { s a m p b u d g e t } } { \mathrm { s a m p b u d g e t } + \operatorname* { m a x } ( q , 0 ) } } \end{array}$ . Then, a random variable $\zeta \sim \mathrm { U n i f o r m } ( 0 , 1 )$ is generated. $\operatorname { I f } \zeta \leq$ sampbudget, the current instance is also selected for labelling. This design allows the strategy to sample more near-boundary instances when the remaining budget is suficient, while automatically reducing supplementary sampling when the budget is tight.

It should be noted that Algorithm 2 only returns the decision signals labelling and corridorFlag. It does not obtain the true label from the oracle and does not update the label sliding window L. The actual oracle query, the insertion of y or Null into $L ,$ and the subsequent model and memory updates are uniformly handled in Algorithm 1 according to the query source. In addition, Algorithm 2 does not directly update 𝛼 and $G$ . When Algorithm  2 returns corridorFlag = True and Algorithm 1 subsequently obtains the true label, the $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \cdot$ -Lite state variables are updated through UpdateBAM<sup>2</sup>ParamsLite. This design keeps the uncertainty-selection module independent from label acquisition and label-window maintenance, and ensures that all active-query labels are routed consistently under the global labelling budget.

## 2.8 Drift‑strength‑aware dual‑memory initialization and soft replacement

This subsection presents SDMI-PDSR, the drift-response module of DMAE. SDMI-PDSR combines drift-strength aware dual-memory initialization with a soft replacement mechanism for candidate classifiers. For each class, DMAE maintains a short-term sample window and a long-term prototype memory, and uses the online-estimated drift strength to adaptively determine their mixing ratio during candidatemodel initialization. The newly initialized classifier is then evaluated through a utility criterion before retention and ensemble replacement, so that memory reconstruction, initialization training, and member updating are coordinated within a structured and controllable procedure.

To leverage both the latest distribution and long-term patterns during initialization, DMAE maintains a twolevel memory structure for each class c. The first level is a short-term sample window $R _ { c }$ with capacity sizesam, which mainly stores representative samples that have been updated under PCN-Weight in a recent period, used to characterize the current distribution and its local decision boundary. The second level is a long-term memory set $H _ { c }$ , which maintains a small number of relatively drift-stable prototype samples via simple representativeness and diversity criteria, used to describe the overall geometry of the class and the long-term contour of minority classes. During online operation, whenever a new labeled sample (x, y, t) arrives, it is first written into $R _ { \mathrm { y } }$ , and $H _ { y }$ is updated when certain representativeness or novelty conditions are satisfied, gradually forming a complementary short-term/long-term dual-memory structure. Short-term memory is highly sensitive to new concepts, whereas long-term memory provides structural constraints from old patterns.

When any ADWIN detector reports significant drift, a drift-intensity index $s _ { d r i f t } \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ is constructed using the mean increment of its error-rate estimates, and a piecewise linear mapping is used to adaptively determine the proportion of short-term memory used in the initialization samples, $\rho ( s _ { d r i f t } )$ , as in Eq. (7).

$$
\rho (s _ {d r i f t}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{c c} \rho_ {m i n}, & s _ {d r i f t} \leq \tau_ {m i n} \\ \rho_ {m i n} + \frac {s _ {d r i f t} - \tau_ {m i n}}{\tau_ {m a x} - \tau_ {m i n}} (\rho_ {m a x} - \rho_ {m i n}), & \tau_ {m i n} <   s _ {d r i f t} <   \tau_ {m a x} \\ \rho_ {m a x}, & s _ {d r i f t} \geq \tau_ {m a x} \end{array} \right.\tag{7}
$$

where $0 < \rho _ { m i n } \leq \rho _ { m a x } \leq 1$ , and $\tau _ { m i n } < \tau _ { m a x }$ control the boundaries between mild, moderate, and severe drift. Intuitively, when $s _ { d r i f t }$ approaches $\tau _ { m i n } , \rho ( s _ { d r i f t } )$ approaches the lower bound $\rho _ { m i n }$ and initialization relies more on long-term memory $H _ { c }$ to preserve historical stable patterns; when $s _ { d r i f t }$ approaches $\tau _ { m a x } , \rho ( s _ { d r i f t } )$ moves toward the upper bound $\rho _ { m a x }$ and initialization relies more on the short-term window $R _ { c }$ to quickly adapt to the new distribution; under moderate drift, $\rho ( s _ { d r i f t } )$ transitions smoothly between the two, providing a tunable trade-of between retention and forgetting.

Given the mixing ratio $\rho ( s _ { d r i f t } )$ , DMAE samples from the short-term window and long-term memory for each class $c \in \{ 1 , \ldots , C \}$ to construct a class-balanced initialization candidate set. Let the current number of valid samples in the short-term window be $\mid R _ { c } \mid ,$ , and let sizesam be the perclass target capacity. Then the number of samples drawn without replacement from $R _ { c }$ is $n _ { c } ^ { ( R ) }$ <sup>)</sup>, defined in Eq. (8).

$$
n _ {c} ^ {(R)} = \left\lfloor \rho (s _ {d r i f t}) \cdot \min (| R _ {c} |, \text { sizesam }) \right\rfloor\tag{8}
$$

To keep the per-class sample size approximately consistent, the number of samples drawn from the long-term memory $H _ { c }$ is $n _ { c } ^ { ( H ) }$ , defined in Eq. (9).

$$
n _ {c} ^ {(H)} = \min (| R _ {c} |, \text {sizesam}) - n _ {c} ^ {(R)}\tag{9}
$$

If $H _ { c }$ contains fewer than $n _ { c } ^ { ( H ) }$ samples, all samples in $H _ { c }$ are selected. Merging the two parts yields the classspecific initialization candidate set $I _ { c }$ as in Eq. (10).

$$
I _ {c} = I _ {c} ^ {\text {short}} \cup I _ {c} ^ {\text {long}}\tag{10}
$$

Then, concatenating candidates across all classes and sorting by arrival time $t _ { i }$ in ascending order yields the global initialization training sequence I as in Eq. (11).

$$
I = \text { SortByTime } \left(\bigcup_ {c = 1} ^ {C} I _ {c}\right)\tag{11}
$$

where each element is recorded as $( x _ { i } , y _ { i } , t _ { i } , \mathrm { d i f f } _ { i } )$ , including the sample features, true class, arrival time, and the comprehensive classification dificulty diff . With this construction, the initialization sequence is approximately balanced across classes, preserves causal order in time, and explicitly carries sample-dificulty information.

After obtaining the time-ordered initialization sequence I, we introduce a time-decay mechanism on top of the comprehensive sample weights. Let $w ( x _ { i } , y _ { i } )$ denote the comprehensive weight computed from classification dificulty and class imbalance ratio (PCN-Weight). For each initialization sample, we introduce an exponentially decaying factor with time as in Eq. (12).

$$
\eta_ {i} = \exp (- \gamma \frac {t _ {\mathrm{now}} - t _ {i}}{\tau})\tag{12}
$$

where $t _ { n o u }$ is the drift time, and $\gamma , \tau > 0$ are hyperparameters controlling the decay slope and time scale, respectively. The new weight $\widetilde { \boldsymbol { w } } _ { i }$ is defined in Eq. (13).

$$
\widetilde {w} _ {i} = w (x _ {i}, y _ {i}) \cdot \eta_ {i}\tag{13}
$$

This design allows the initialization stage to still leverage historical minority samples to stabilize the decision boundary, while automatically emphasizing recent samples, in particular recent hard minority samples, thereby providing a controllable trade-of between memory retention and rapid adaptation.

Next, the algorithm adopts an online-bagging style initialization training for the new base classifier $C _ { n e w }$ over sequence I. For each sample $( \mathbf { x } _ { i } , y _ { i } , \widetilde { w } _ { i } )$ , a repetition count is generated from a Poisson distribution $k _ { i } \sim \mathrm { P o i s s o n } ( \widetilde { w } _ { i } )$ , and the replicated samples are used to perform several incremental updates on $C _ { n e w }$ . Consequently, the new base classifier is initialized under three constraints, namely class balance, dificulty awareness, and time decay, so that from the beginning it can jointly accommodate recent distribution adaptation and long-term pattern inheritance, thereby substantially alleviating the cold-start issue induced by drift response. At this point, SDMI completes the dual-memory-based initialization process conditioned on the drift intensity.

Although SDMI significantly improves initialization quality, two risks remain if the conventional hard replacement rule is still applied, that is, directly replacing the existing member with the largest ADWIN error by $C _ { n e w } .$ . First, the generalization ability of the new model under the new distribution has not been suficiently validated; good performance on the initialization samples may not persist on subsequent data. Second, the replaced old model may still provide irreplaceable contributions in minority regions or in ensemble diversity; eliminating members purely by overall error can mistakenly remove components that are crucial to robustness. Therefore, DMAE assigns a finite trial period to the new model and compares new and old members using a utility score driven by performance and diversity.

Specifically, suppose the current ensemble contains D base classifiers $\{ \bar { C _ { k } } \} _ { k = 1 } ^ { D }$ . Over a recent labeled window, a comprehensive utility score $U _ { k }$ is maintained for each base classifier as in Eq. (14).

$$
U _ {k} = \lambda_ {a c c} (1 - \widehat {e} ^ {k}) + \lambda_ {i m b} \widehat {G M e a n} ^ {k} + \lambda_ {d i v} \widehat {\mathrm{Div}} ^ {k}\tag{14}
$$

where $\widehat { e } ^ { k }$ is the error rate on the sliding window characterizing overall accuracy; $\widehat { G M e a n } ^ { \prime }$ is the minority-oriented G-Mean on the same window measuring adaptation to class imbalance; $\overline { { \mathrm { D i v } } } ^ { + }$ denotes the disagreement degree between this member and other members’ predictions (larger indicates higher diversity); and $\lambda _ { a c c } , \lambda _ { i m b } , \lambda _ { d i \nu } \geq 0$ are weights controlling the relative importance of the three terms.

After SDMI training, $C _ { n e w }$ does not immediately replace any old member. Instead, it is softly inserted into the ensemble, forming a temporary ensemble of size $D + 1$ participates in prediction with a small initial weight, and initializes its utility score $U _ { n e w } = 0$ along with a trial counter. Then, during a trial period of length $T _ { t r i a l } .$ , all newly arriving labeled samples are used both for routine model updates and for utility-statistics updates; on a unified sliding window, each classifier’s $\widehat { e } ^ { k }$ , GMean<sup>̂</sup> , $\widehat { \mathrm { D i v } } ^ { k }$ , and $U _ { k }$ are updated synchronously. This design allows each label to support $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \cdot$ Lite threshold adjustment and SDMI memory maintenance, while also providing evidence for PDSR utility evaluation, thereby maximizing the use of label information under a fixed labeling budget.

When the trial period ends, PDSR finds the index with the smallest utility score among all $D + 1$ members: $k ^ { * } = \underset { k } { \mathrm { a r g m i n } } U _ { k } , \mathrm { I f } k ^ { * }$ ≠ new, then the candidate model $C _ { n e w }$ is better than at least one existing member under the joint measure of overall accuracy, minority performance, and diversity, and $C _ { n e w }$ replaces $C _ { k ^ { * } }$ , restoring the ensemble size to D. If $k ^ { * } = \mathrm { n e w }$ , the candidate model fails to provide suficient gain and is discarded, keeping the original ensemble unchanged. With the combined SDMI–PDSR mechanism, a new base classifier must pass a statistical trial screening that simultaneously considers overall accuracy, minority performance, and diversity before becoming a formal ensemble member, thereby significantly reducing the risk of catastrophic reset caused by drift mis-detection or short-term oscillations.

SDMI-PDSR is not independent of the uncertainty and querying strategy described earlier; it shares the same driftintensity index $s _ { d r i f t }$ , the same label sliding window, and the same dual-level sample memory structure with $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } { \cdot } \mathbf { L i t e }$ The uncertainty strategy uses $s _ { d r i f t }$ to adaptively adjust classpair thresholds; SDMI uses the same index to balance inheritance and forgetting between short-term samples and longterm prototypes; PDSR determines which base classifiers to retain under a unified utility scale. Together, the three components form a closed loop that answers, respectively, which samples to query, which memories to use for initializing new models, and which base classifiers to keep, providing a stable and eficient online response mechanism to mixed concept drift in multiclass imbalanced data streams.

Compared with hard replacement strategies that rely solely on error rate, this soft replacement mechanism explicitly considers global accuracy, minority-class performance, and predictive diversity when introducing a new model, efectively reducing the risk of catastrophic replacement caused by drift misdetection or short-term fluctuations, while preserving structural stability as the ensemble converges, thereby providing DMAE with a smoother and more reliable structural selfadaptation capability in long-horizon multiclass-imbalanced streams with mixed concept drift.

## 2.9 Comprehensive sample weighting with PCN‑weight

This subsection presents PCN-Weight, a comprehensive sample-weighting formulation for DMAE. PCN-Weight jointly incorporates local prediction dificulty, intra-class structural information, class-imbalance status, and time decay into a unified weighting framework. In this way, it strengthens hard samples near decision boundaries, distinguishes typical patterns from tail samples within the same class, and limits the long-term dominance of majority classes during incremental training.

Given the sample $( x , y )$ arriving at the current time, the ensemble classifier outputs the class posterior probability distribution for x. Let the class with the largest predicted probability be $y _ { c _ { 1 } }$ and the second largest be $y _ { c _ { \gamma } }$ . Define the decision-boundary margin as margi $n ( x ) = p ( \bar { y _ { c _ { 1 } } } \mid x ) - p ( y _ { c _ { 2 } } \mid x )$ To distinguish correctly predicted high-confidence samples, near-boundary samples, and clearly misclassified samples, we introduce indicator variables $t f$ and s. According to the relationship among the true label y and $y _ { c _ { 1 } } , y _ { c _ { 2 } }$ , they are defined as in Eq. (15).

$$
\begin{array}{l} t f = 1, s = 0, \qquad \text {when y_{c_{1}} = y}, \\ t f = - 1, s = 1, \qquad \text {when y_{c_{2}} = y}, \\ t f = - 1, s = 0, \text {when y_{c_{1}} \neq y and y_{c_{2}} \neq y}. \end{array}\tag{15}
$$

Based on this, the base dificulty term of the sample (x, y) is defined as in Eq. (16).

$$
\mathrm{diff} _ {b a s e} (x, y) = (1 - t f \cdot m a r g i n (x)) \times \exp (1 - t f - s)\tag{16}
$$

When a sample is correctly predicted with high confidence, margin(x) is large and $t f = 1$ , and thus $\mathrm { d i f f } _ { b a s e } ( x , y )$ takes a small value; when a sample lies near the boundary or is misclassified, margin(x) is small or even negative and $t f = - 1 .$ , and $\mathrm { d i f f } _ { b a s e } ( x , y )$ will be significantly amplified, thereby emphasizing the hard samples that truly shape the decision boundary at the weighting level.

To characterize the class-imbalance status within the current label sliding window, let the window size be sizelab, the number of placeholders (i.e., positions where no label is requested) be numNull, the number of true labels of class y within the window be labelnum , and the total number of classes be C. The imbalance indicator of class y is defined as in Eq. (17).

$$
\mathrm{imb} _ {y} = \frac {\text { labelnum } _ {y}}{(s i z e l a b - n u m N u l l) / C}\tag{17}
$$

When im $\mathsf { b } _ { \mathrm { y } } = 1$ , the occurrence proportion of class y in the window is close to the average level; imb $_ \mathrm { v } < 1$ 1 indicates a relatively minority class, and im $) _ { \mathrm { y } } > 1$ indicates a relatively majority class. This indicator provides a quantitative basis for subsequent class-quota control and minority amplification.

Relying on the short-term window and long-term memory structure constructed previously, a long-term prototype vector $\mu _ { c }$ is maintained for each class c, which can be obtained by a weighted average over representative samples in the long-term memory set of that class. Let h(x) denote the representation of sample x under the current ensemble (e.g., the last-layer latent vector). Then the Euclidean distance of $( x , y )$ to the prototype of its class is defined as in Eq. (18).

$$
d _ {p r o t o} (x, y) = \parallel h (x) - \mu_ {y} \parallel_ {2}\tag{18}
$$

To reflect the typical spatial scale of class y at the current stage, a smoothed average prototype distance $d _ { \mathrm { v } } ( t )$ is maintained for each class, as in Eq. (19). Let the smoothing coeficient be $\lambda _ { d } \in ( 0 , 1 ]$ . When a new sample $( x , y )$ arrives and $y = c ,$ , update $d _ { y } ( t )$ by

$$
d _ {y} (t) = (1 - \lambda_ {d}) d _ {y} (t - 1) + \lambda_ {d} d _ {p r o t o} (x, y)\tag{19}
$$

For other classes, $d _ { c } ( t )$ remains unchanged. Based on this, the prototype-contrast ratio is defined as in Eq. (20).

$$
r (x, y) = \frac {d _ {p r o t o} (x , y)}{d _ {y} (t)}\tag{20}
$$

When $r ( x , y ) > 1$ , the sample is significantly farther from the class prototype, typically corresponding to tail hard examples or structurally atypical samples; when $r ( x , y ) < 1$ the sample is closer to the prototype and represents a more typical intra-class pattern. Through $r ( x , y )$ , PCN-Weight distinguishes typical samples near the prototype from structural tail samples deviating from the prototype, and relatively amplifies the latter in weight allocation, so that under a limited labeling budget the method focuses more on instances that are more sensitive to intra-class boundary geometry.

Relying only on the instantaneous proportions within the sliding window may still cause short-term high-frequency majority classes to continuously occupy excessive training capacity over a long horizon. To explicitly introduce a class-level quota constraint into the sample-weight design, PCN-Weight defines a quota adjustment factor q(y) for each class based on imb , as in Eq. (21).

$$
q (y) = 1 + \lambda_ {q} \left(\frac {1}{\mathrm{imb} _ {y}} - 1\right)\tag{21}
$$

where $\lambda _ { q } \in ( 0 , 1 )$ controls the adjustment magnitude, and in implementation q(y) is clipped to the interval $[ q _ { m i n } , q _ { m a x } ]$ where $0 < q _ { m i n } < 1 < q _ { m a x }$ . When imb > 1, the quota factor $q ( y ) < 1$ , and the total efective weight of this class over a period is mildly compressed; when imb $< 1 , q ( y ) > 1$ reserving more efective training quota for minority classes, thereby suppressing the long-term dominance of majority classes in parameter updates over the stream.

Considering that in streaming scenarios sample usefulness gradually decays, each sample involved in subsequent training and updates records its arrival time $t _ { a r r i \nu i n g } ,$ , so that time decay can be introduced in PCN-Weight and in the dual-memory initialization process. Let the current time step be $t _ { c u r r e n t }$ . The time-decay factor is defined as in Eq. (22).

$$
f a c t o r (x) = \exp \left(- \gamma \cdot \frac {t _ {\text { current }} - t _ {\text { arriving }}}{\text { size } l a b}\right)\tag{22}
$$

where $\gamma > 0$ controls the decay rate. This factor makes the weights of samples close to the current time approach 1, while the weights of older historical samples exponentially decay with time, which better matches the temporal evolution of concepts and prevents outdated samples from exerting overly strong influence after drift.

Combining the above four components, PCN-Weight integrates base dificulty, intra-class prototype contrast, class-level quota, and time decay into an overall weighting function. Given a sample (x, y), the final training weight is defined as in Eq. (23).

$$
\operatorname{weight} _ {P C N} (x, y) = \log \left(1 + \operatorname{diff} _ {\text {base}} (x, y) + \frac {1}{\operatorname{imb} _ {y}}\right) \cdot \left[ 1 + \lambda_ {p} (r (x, y) - 1) \right] \cdot q (y) \cdot f a c t o r (x)\tag{23}
$$

where $\lambda _ { p } \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ controls the strength of the prototype-contrast term. In the final training weight, the first part characterizes classification dificulty in the margin space and further amplifies minority samples via $1 / \mathrm { i m b _ { y } }$ ; the second part uses $r ( x , y )$ to adaptively identify and emphasize intraclass tail samples far from the prototype, so that samples that truly afect intra-class boundary geometry receive higher weights; the third part imposes a soft quota at the class level according to imbalance status to prevent majority classes from dominating training over long horizons; and the fourth part uses time decay to keep the model focused on the recent distribution. With this comprehensive weighting design, DMAE focuses on hard and critical samples locally, and balances the efective contribution of each class across time globally, providing more robust and discriminative gradient signals for the subsequent active labeling strategy and drift adaptation mechanisms.

## 2.10 Time and space complexity analysis

We analyze the time and space complexity of the proposed DMAE algorithm as follows. Let the stream length be n, the number of base classifiers in the ensemble be D, and the number of classes be C. Denote the per-sample prediction cost and the per-sample incremental update cost of a single base classifier as $T _ { \mathrm { p r e d } }$ and $T _ { \mathrm { u p d } } .$ , respectively. Let the label sliding window length be $W _ { \mathrm { l a b } } ;$ for each class c, let the capacity of the short-term sample window $R _ { c }$ be $W _ { \mathrm { s h o r t } }$ ; let the maximum number of prototypes per class stored in the long-term prototype memory $H _ { c }$ be $P _ { \mathrm { p r o t o } } ;$ and let the length of the PDSR trial period be $T _ { \mathrm { t r i a l } } .$ . For the space analysis, let the model size upper bound of a single base learner be $S _ { \mathrm { b a s e } } .$

(1) Time Complexity: For each arriving unlabeled instance, DMAE first performs ensemble prediction over D base classifiers and computes the uncertainty information used by BAM<sup>2</sup>-Lite. The prediction-stage cost is $O ( D \cdot T _ { \mathrm { p r e d } } )$ . The class counts in the label sliding window are maintained incrementally, so estimating the current class distribution and imbalance state does not require scanning the whole stream. The $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } -$ Lite decision only involves the current top-1 and top-2 predicted classes, the corresponding classpair state, the current drift-strength scalar, and the current budget ratio. Therefore, its per-sample decision cost is constant with respect to the stream length n. Under a fixed labelling budget b, only a fraction of arriving instances can trigger oracle labelling and supervised updating. For a queried instance, DMAE computes the PCN-Weight, updates the corresponding short-term sample window and long-term prototype memory, updates the $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } -$ Lite state if the sample falls into the corridor, and incrementally updates the ensemble. The dominant supervised update cost is $O ( D \cdot T _ { \mathrm { u p d } } )$ . Therefore, when no drift response is triggered, the expected per-sample time cost can be written as $O ( D \cdot T _ { \mathrm { p r e d } } + b D \cdot T _ { \mathrm { u p d } } )$ , where the factor b reflects that supervised updating is performed only on budgeted queried samples. Since b is fixed in the experiments, the regular online processing cost grows linearly with the stream length. When ADWIN reports significant concept drift, the SDMI-PDSR subroutine is triggered. In SDMI, for each class, at most $W _ { \mathrm { s h o r t } }$ samples from the short-term window and $P _ { \mathrm { p r o t o } }$ prototypes from the long-term memory are used to construct the initialization sequence. The cost of initializing the candidate base classifier is therefore $O \big ( C ( W _ { \mathrm { s h o r t } } + P _ { \mathrm { p r o t o } } ) \cdot T _ { \mathrm { u p d } } \big )$ During the PDSR trial period, the candidate classifier is evaluated together with the existing ensemble on labelled samples. The trial-stage overhead is bounded by $O \big ( T _ { \mathrm { t r i a l } } ( D \cdot T _ { \mathrm { p r e d } } + T _ { \mathrm { u p d } } ) \big )$ . Let $N _ { \mathrm { d r i f t } }$ denote the number of detected drift events over the stream. The total time complexity can be expressed as $O \big ( n ( D \cdot T _ { \mathrm { p r e d } } + b D \cdot T _ { \mathrm { u p d } } ) \big ) + O \big ( N _ { \mathrm { d r i f t } } \cdot C ( W _ { \mathrm { s h o r t } } + P _ { \mathrm { p r o t o } } ) \cdot T _ { \mathrm { u p d } } \big )$

$+ O \big ( N _ { \mathrm { d r i f t } } \cdot T _ { \mathrm { t r i a l } } ( D \cdot T _ { \mathrm { p r e d } } + T _ { \mathrm { u p d } } ) \big )$ This expression shows that the main online cost is linear in n, while the additional SDMI-PDSR cost is incurred only when drift is detected. In long-horizon streams, DMAE does not revisit historical samples and does not store the full stream. Therefore, the regular processing cost remains linear. Under frequent drift, the extra cost is controlled by the fixed memory capacities $W _ { \mathrm { s h o r t } }$ and $P _ { \mathrm { p r o t o } } .$ , the fixed ensemble size $D ,$ and the bounded trial length $T _ { \mathrm { t r i a l } } .$ Consequently, even in drift-intensive streams, the additional cost is bounded by the number of detected drift events and does not require full-history retraining.

(2) Space Complexity: The major memory consumption of DMAE comes from the ensemble model, the label sliding window, the class-wise short-term sample windows, the long-term prototype memories, and the BAM<sup>2</sup>-Lite state variables. For a given base-learner family, storing D base classifiers requires $O ( D \cdot S _ { \mathrm { b a s e } } )$ space. The label sliding window of length $W _ { \mathrm { l a b } }$ requires $O ( W _ { \mathrm { l a b } } )$ space. The short-term windows and long-term prototype memories are maintained for each class, resulting in the memory cost $O \big ( C ( W _ { \mathrm { s h o r t } } + P _ { \mathrm { p r o t o } } ) \big )$ The $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } \cdot \mathbf { I }$ Lite state includes the class-level potential vector and class-pair interaction statistics. These structures have dimensions no larger than C or $C \times C$ leading to $O ( C ^ { 2 } )$ space. Other auxiliary variables, such as the drift-strength index, budget counters, ADWIN statistics, and PDSR utility statistics, require only bounded storage with respect to the stream length. Therefore, the overall space complexity of DMAE is $O \big ( D \cdot S _ { \mathrm { b a s e } } + W _ { \mathrm { l a b } } + C ( W _ { \mathrm { s h o r t } } + P _ { \mathrm { p r o t o } } ) + C ^ { 2 } \big )$ . Since $D ,$ $C , W _ { \mathrm { l a b } } , W _ { \mathrm { s h o r t } } , P _ { \mathrm { p r o t o } } ,$ and $T _ { \mathrm { t r i a l } }$ are fixed hyperparameters independent of the stream length $n ,$ the space usage of DMAE does not grow with $n .$ This is important for long-horizon streaming scenarios: DMAE maintains only bounded sliding windows, bounded prototype memories, fixed-size ensemble members, and fixedsize class-pair statistics, rather than storing all historical data. Thus, the method is practical for continuous data streams with limited memory resources.

Evaluation measures for multiclass imbalanced data streams can be broadly divided into class-wise metrics and integral metrics (Brzezinski et al. 2020). Class-wise metrics, such as Recall, Accuracy, and the corresponding F-score, characterize a model’s recognition ability and error structure from the perspective of individual classes. By contrast, integral metrics aggregate multiclass performance into a single indicator, such as G-Mean, and place greater emphasis on maintaining balanced recognition quality across classes.

It should be noted that Accuracy and F-score are often sensitive to class priors. When class proportions fluctuate over time, these measures may vary substantially even if the model’s intrinsic discriminative ability does not change materially, thereby producing apparent performance oscillations caused by distribution shifts rather than true learning dynamics. In comparison, Recall directly measures the coverage of correctly recognized instances for the target class and is therefore more interpretable in multiclass imbalanced settings with asymmetric costs. Accordingly, a reliable online learner should maintain both high and stable recall performance over time.

Based on these considerations, we use Accuracy, Kappa, G-Mean, Recall, and their overall average performance as the main evaluation metrics. Accuracy, Kappa, G-Mean, and Recall are updated instance by instance during online learning, whereas the overall average performance is computed after training by aggregating the prediction records over the full online process.

## 3.2 Implementation details and hyperparameter settings

## 3.3 Reproducibility and fairness of comparison

Some of the baseline algorithms considered in this study have previously reported experimental results on related data streams in the literature. However, the experimental configurations used in those studies are not always identical to the setting adopted in this work. In particular, diferences may arise in the evaluation protocol, labeling budget, stream generation settings, and preprocessing procedures. To ensure a fair and reproducible comparison, we do not directly reuse the performance numbers reported in previous publications. Instead, all baseline algorithms are re-evaluated under a unified experimental environment that is consistent with the online active-learning scenario considered in this paper. Specifically, all algorithms are evaluated using the same interleaved test-then-train (prequential) evaluation protocol and operate under the same labeling budget constraint. For synthetic streams, identical generator configurations and drift settings are used for all methods, ensuring that the imbalance evolution and concept-drift patterns are consistent across algorithms. In addition, all methods are executed within the same MOA framework and follow the same preprocessing and random-seed settings whenever applicable. Because earlier studies may adopt diferent evaluation protocols, supervision levels, or stream configurations, the absolute numerical values reported in this paper may difer from those originally reported in the literature. Nevertheless, re-evaluating all methods under the same controlled conditions ensures that the comparisons presented here are consistent, reproducible, and fair. To reduce the influence of random fluctuations, all stochastic components are controlled by fixed random seeds, and the final reported values are averaged over repeated runs whenever the corresponding method contains random operations. This setting improves the stability of the reported comparisons and avoids drawing conclusions from a single random execution.

Below we briefly summarize the core ideas and the primary focus of each baseline. The first category consists of imbalance oriented methods, which aim to mitigate learning bias caused by the scarcity of minority class instances. The robust online self adjusting ensemble ROSE improves component diversity via random feature subspaces and dynamically maintains training information through class wise sliding windows and a self adjustment mechanism, enabling strong robustness under high imbalance ratios and gradual drifts. The multi class oversampling online bagging method MOOB intro duces a time decayed oversampling strategy into the online bagging framework, increasing minority class exposure dur ing incremental training and thus improving learnability for rare categories. CSMOTE dynamically generates synthetic minority class samples to maintain within block class bal ance, which can partially resist minority boundary collapse under drift, but it often incurs higher computational cost and is more sensitive to sample quality in high dimensional or complex distributions. The second category includes drift adaptive methods, whose core lies in rapid perception of distributional changes and timely model updating. The Kappa updated ensemble KUE weights ensemble members using the Kappa statistic and adjusts member contributions driven by performance feedback, which helps maintain adap tivity during transitions from stationary to drifting phases, while its feedback reliability becomes more sensitive under severe class imbalance. SRP uses a pruned reservoir to con trol resource usage and combines explicit drift detection to trigger updates, ofering strong practicality in resource constrained streaming environments, although its mechanism is not specifically designed for multi class imbalance. The cost sensitive adaptive random forest CSARF incorporates cost sensitive learning into tree modeling and ensemble updating, leveraging class distribution information to balance over all accuracy and minority recognition ability. It is suitable for complex drift scenarios, yet it may still face adaptation pressure when abrupt drifts coincide with drastic changes in multi class structure. The third category comprises hybrid and active learning methods, which emphasize improving label utilization eficiency and overall performance under limited labeling budgets. CALMID selects high information instances via an uncertainty threshold driven querying mechanism and combines imbalance oriented strategies to enable more targeted learning, making it suitable for label scarce multi class data streams, although its efectiveness often depends on threshold settings and how class dificulty is characterized. The MicFoal framework can dynamically adjust the labeling budget according to the stream status to accommodate changes in the number of classes or fluctuations in task dificulty. AdaOB combines online bagging with dynamic resampling and strengthens minority class representation through an adaptive mechanism. OALM-MI assigns higher attention to rare events via incremental weight updating, thereby improving minority recall, but its systematic capability for drift adaptation is relatively limited. In summary, these baseline methods form a complementary comparison suite from three perspectives, imbalance correction, drift adaptation, and label eficiency, providing a solid and representative reference for validating DMAE in terms of active querying, drift responsiveness, and robustness to multi class imbalance.

## 3.7 Parameter sensitivity analysis

To examine the parameter sensitivity of the proposed DMAE, this subsection reports the results of a set of parameter sensitivity experiments. The basic idea is to vary one target parameter while keeping all other parameters unchanged. For a fair comparison and to keep the computational cost controllable, the ensemble size of all ensemble methods is fixed to 10. The investigated parameters include the baseline uncertainty threshold θ, the mixing weight β between $\mathrm { B A M } ^ { 2 }$ and the baseline threshold, the marginal corridor width δ in $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 }$ , the upper bound of the short term memory ratio under strong drift $\rho _ { m a x } ,$ and the global scaling factor of long term prototypes $\lambda _ { l o n g } .$ The parameter sensitivity experiments are conducted on the synthetic data stream VarImb GIS-GrN10 5. The default parameter setting is θ equals 0.50, β equals 0.50, δ equals 0.05, $\rho _ { m a x }$ equals 0.80, and $\lambda _ { l o n g }$ equals 0.80. Tables 8 and 9 present the results of the parameter sensitivity experiments.

As shown in the tables, in data stream environments with multi class imbalance accompanied by concept drift, DMAE exhibits strong overall robustness with respect to the five key hyperparameters. By considering Tables 8 and 9 together, it can be seen that when adjusting the baseline uncertainty threshold, the $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 }$ mixing weight and corrido width, as well as the upper bound of the short term memory ratio and the long term prototype scaling factor in SDMI within the preset ranges, Accuracy, Kappa, G-Mean, and Recall all remain at a high level with only limited fluctuations, indicating that the performance is mainly determined by the proposed structural design rather than depending on delicate hyperparameter tuning. For the baseline uncertainty threshold theta, we observe that when theta increases gradually from a smaller value to a moderate level, the four metrics generally show a trend of improving first and then stabilizing, reaching a relatively high level around the moderate threshold; when theta continues to increase, the metrics only weaken slightly, suggesting that neither overly aggressive nor overly conservative querying strategies are advantageous, while a moderate threshold achieves a more reasonable balance between the number of labeled samples and their dificulty, so that the selected instances provide suficient information gain without substantially increasing labeling cost, which is consistent with the proposed margin based active learning rationale. For the $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 }$ mixing weight beta, as beta increases from weak intervention to a moderate strength, G-Mean and Recall, especially the minority class related metrics, improve more noticeably, indicating that within this range, threshold correction guided by class pair statistics and drift intensity can efectively enhance discrimination near the decision boundary; when beta further increases, the four metrics decrease slightly but the overall level remains high, implying that overly strong correction may weaken the stabilizing role of the original threshold, yet it does not disrupt the overall performance profile. The vari ation trend of the marginal corridor width delta reflects the necessity of activating $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 }$ only near the decision boundary: when the corridor expands from extremely narrow to a moderate width, the four metrics rise simultaneously, suggesting that more truly ambiguous samples receive class pair calibration; when delta continues to widen, performance drops slightly, indicating that frequent correction on high confidence samples provides no additional benefit and may instead introduce mild fluctuations. The results for the upper bound of the short term memory ratio under strong drift, rho max, show that increasing rho max from a small value to a moderate level leads to faster responses to abrupt concept changes, with clear improvements in G-Mean and Recall; when rho max becomes even larger, the metrics tend to level of or decline slightly, indicating that excessive reliance on the most recent window weakens the utilization of periodic and stable patterns. The long term prototype scaling factor lambda long reflects the regularization strength of long term memory: when lambda long increases from a small value to a moderate level, Accuracy and Kappa improve continuously and the decision boundary becomes smoother and more stable; when lambda long is too large, the improvement in classification performance becomes limited while the querying ratio rises markedly, suggesting that overly strong long term constraints reduce the eficiency of absorbing new concepts. Overall, these five parameters follow a consistent pattern in which moderate values are generally better, while the two extremes are slightly weaker but the overall performance remains stable, and the observed trends match the expected behavior of the localized threshold correction mechanism in $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 }$ Lite and the dual memory design in SDMI. In general, the performance changes only marginally when tuning these parameters, therefore DMAE is not particularly sensi tive to their settings.

## 3.8 Ablation study on the components of DMAE

To examine the contribution of the major components of DMAE, we conduct a progressive component-level ablation study on the core modules of the framework. Instead of changing the base classifier or the evaluation protocol, all variants are evaluated under the same online active-learning setting, the same labelling budget, and the same data stream. Starting from a simplified active-learning-based online ensemble, we gradually add the main components of DMAE and observe how the online performance changes over time. This design provides a direct way to assess whether each major module brings additional benefits when it is incorporated into the framework. The compared variants are defined as follows. DMAE-a denotes the base active learner, which removes BAM<sup>2</sup>-Lite, SDMI-PDSR, and PCN-Weight, and retains only the basic active online ensemble update. DMAE-a-b adds $\mathbf { B A M } ^ { 2 } -$ Lite to DMAE-a, so as to examine the efect of the hybrid uncertainty estimation and querying mechanism. DMAE-a-b-c further adds SDMI-PDSR, which introduces drift-strength-aware dual-memory initialization and trial-based soft replacement. DMAE denotes the full model, which additionally includes PCN-Weight for comprehensive sample weighting. In this way, the ablation study focuses on the incremental efect of three major components: BAM<sup>2</sup>-Lite -Lite, SDMI-PDSR, and PCN-Weight.

All variants are evaluated on the synthetic data stream VarImb\_SVg\_2, which contains both evolving class imbalance and concept drift. This stream is suitable for observing whether each component contributes to online adaptation under dynamic imbalance. Figure 8 reports the temporal performance curves of the four variants as the number of processed instances increases. As shown in Fig. 8, DMAEa obtains the lowest performance among the four variants in most stages of the stream. This result indicates that a basic active online ensemble is not suficient to handle multiclass imbalance and concept drift under a limited labelling budget. After incorporating BAM<sup>2</sup>-Lite, DMAE-a-b shows a visible improvement over DMAE-a. This suggests that the hybrid uncertainty and querying mechanism helps allocate limited labels to more informative boundary regions, thereby improving the quality of labelled samples used for online updating. When SDMI-PDSR is further introduced, DMAE-a-b-c achieves higher and relatively more stable performance than DMAE-a-b, especially around the middle part of the stream where the performance curves show more fluctuation. This observation suggests that drift-strength-aware dual-memory initialization and trial-based soft replacement can help the ensemble adapt after distributional changes and reduce the negative efect of abrupt model replacement. Finally, the full DMAE model achieves the highest curve among the four variants in most stages. This additional improvement indicates that PCN-Weight further enhances the use of queried samples by assigning stronger training emphasis to dificult and minority-relevant instances.

Overall, the progressive ablation results in Fig. 8 show that the three major components of DMAE are complementary rather than redundant. BAM<sup>2</sup>-Lite mainly improves annotation efficiency and boundary-focused querying; SDMI-PDSR contributes to drift response and ensemble evolution; and PCN-Weight further strengthens weighted adaptation to hard and minority-relevant samples. Although this experiment focuses on component-level ablation rather than isolating every internal operation of each module, the consistent performance gains across the progressive variants provide evidence that the main modules jointly contribute to the final performance of DMAE under drifting multiclass imbalanced data streams.

## 3.8.1 Statistical significance analysis

This subsection reports the statistical significance analysis based on the Accuracy, Kappa, G-Mean, and Recall results of all methods on the 20 experimental data streams, involving a total of 11 algorithms. For each dataset, the performance values of the compared algorithms are first obtained from the online evaluation process and then converted into ranks. The statistical analysis is therefore conducted on the original per-dataset performance values rather than on aggregated averages, ensuring that the variability across datasets is properly considered in the statistical inference. The average ranks of all algorithms across the datasets with respect to the four evaluation metrics are summarized in Table 10, where a lower average rank indicates better overall performance. As shown in the table, DMAE consistently achieves the best average rank across all metrics, whereas CSMOTE ranks last. To determine whether the observed diferences are statistically significant rather than caused by random variations across datasets, we adopt a non-parametric statistical testing framework commonly used for algorithm comparison across multiple datasets (Demšar 2006). Compared with parametric tests, non-parametric tests do not assume a specific distribution of the performance values and are therefore more suitable for evaluating multiple algorithms under heterogeneous data-stream environments.

Following this framework, we first apply an omnibus significance test to examine whether there exist overall performance diferences among the compared algorithms. Although the Friedman test is the classical non-parametric approach for multi-algorithm comparison, it is often considered relatively conservative when the number of algorithms and datasets is limited. Therefore, we employ the Iman–Davenport test, which can be regarded as an improved version of the Friedman test and provides a more accurate F-distribution approximation while preserving the same rank-based comparison principle. The null hypothesis assumes that there is no significant performance diference among the compared algorithms. If the null hypothesis is rejected, we further conduct the Nemenyi post-hoc test to determine whether the performance of DMAE difers significantly from that of the other competing methods.

Table  11 reports the results of the Iman Davenport test. At the 0.05 significance level, the critical value is obtained from the F distribution with 10 and 190 degrees of freedom. For Accuracy, Kappa, G-Mean, and Recall, the Iman Davenport statistics are all larger than their corresponding critical values, indicating that the diferences in algorithm ranks are statistically significant. We then perform the Nemenyi post hoc test, where the critical difference is 2.655. To keep the critical diference diagrams readable and to focus on the most competitive methods, we present only the top ten ranked algorithms, since these methods show stronger adaptability, faster recovery, and more stable performance than CSMOTE across a variety of concept drift settings, and including CSMOTE would provide limited additional insight while reducing figure clarity. Figures 9, 10, 11, and 12 show the Nemenyi test results for Accuracy, Kappa, G-Mean, and Recall, respectively. The diagrams indicate that DMAE is not significantly diferent from OALM-MI, whereas it significantly outperforms the other nine algorithms.

## 4 Conclusions

This study addresses the coupled challenge of multiclass imbalance and concept drift in online active learning. DMAE is designed to coordinate budgeted uncertainty querying, drift-aware memory reconstruction, soft ensemble replacement, and weighted online updating within a unified framework. From the perspective of uncertainty modeling, DMAE decomposes the conventional single margin-based querying rule into a global baseline threshold and a class-pair-level adaptive correction provided by BAM<sup>2</sup>-Lite. The baseline threshold preserves the general structure of the original decision rule, while the class-pair correction is activated within a predefined margin corridor and adjusts the efective threshold according to class-pair information, imbalance structure, and drift intensity. This design encourages the model to query informative bound ary samples, especially those related to minority classes, while avoiding excessive label consumption on high-confidence instances. For drift adaptation, the SDMI module balances short-term and long-term memory according to the estimated drift strength. When the drift signal is weak, the candidate classifier inherits more information from long-term prototypes so that relatively stable historical patterns can be preserved. When the drift signal becomes stronger, the short-term window receives more emphasis, allowing the candidate classifier to adapt more quickly to the newly emerging distribution. Combined with the PDSR-based trial replacement strategy, DMAE avoids directly switching between full model reset and completely fixed ensemble structures. Instead, it evaluates candidate classifiers before replacement, thereby improving the stability of ensemble evolution under changing data distributions. For online updating, PCN-Weight connects the queried samples with the incremental training process by assigning larger weights to hard, minority-relevant, and structurally informative instances. In this way, samples emphasized during the querying stage can also receive suficient attention during model updating. This mechanism helps reduce the mismatch between active query selection and imbalance-aware learning, which is a common issue in multiclass imbalanced data streams under limited labels. Experimental results on synthetic and real-world data streams show that DMAE achieves competitive and generally stable performance compared with representative sampling methods, cost-sensitive ensemble methods, drift-adaptive methods, and active-learning baselines. The results on multiple metrics, average ranks, ablation analysis, parameter sensitivity analysis, and statistica tests indicate that the proposed components contribute to improving label utilization, minority-class recognition, and adaptation to evolving distributions. These findings suggest that jointly modeling uncertainty, memory, and sample weighting is beneficial for online active learning in multiclass imbalanced concept-drifting data streams. This study also has several limitations. First, DMAE relies on random-query labels in the label sliding window to estimate the current class distribution. When the labelling budget is extremely small, this estimate may become less stable, especially for rare classes. Second, although the memory sizes and PDSR trial length are bounded, fre quent drift events may still introduce additional computational overhead due to repeated candidate initialization and trial evaluation. Third, the current framework is mainly evaluated with traditional online ensemble learners, and its behavior with deep streaming models or more complex feature representations requires further investigation. Finally, the present experiments focus on component-level ablation, while more fine-grained analysis of the internal operations of each module could provide deeper insight into the contribution of each design choice

Future work will focus on improving the eficiency and adaptability of DMAE in more demanding online environments. One direction is to further reduce the computational overhead of BAM<sup>2</sup>-Lite and SDMI-PDSR, so that the method can better fit high-frequency and resourceconstrained streaming scenarios. Another direction is to develop more adaptive budget allocation strategies, allowing the labelling budget to be adjusted according to drift severity, class imbalance, and model uncertainty. It is also valuable to extend the decomposable asymmetric margin and dual-memory principles to more complex tasks, such as multilabel learning, graph-structured data streams, and deep streaming models. These extensions may further improve the applicability of uncertainty-aware active ensemble learning in real-world dynamic environments.
