# TIC-GRPO: Provable and Efficient Optimization for Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

Lei Pang <sup>1</sup> Jun Luo <sup>2</sup> Ruinan Jin <sup>2</sup> <sup>3</sup>

## Abstract

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), recently introduced by DeepSeek, is a critic-free reinforcement learning algorithm for fine-tuning large language models. GRPO replaces the value function in Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with group-normalized rewards while retaining PPO-style token-level importance sampling based on an old policy. Our theoretical analysis reveals that the GRPO update rule estimates the policy gradient at the old policy rather than the current one; however, since the old policy is refreshed every few steps, the resulting discrepancy remains small and the induced bias is negligible in practice. To empirically validate this insight, we conduct an ablation study that entirely removes importance sampling and performs multiple optimization steps using gradients estimated at a fixed old policy. Remarkably, this simplified variant attains performance comparable to standard GRPO.

Motivated by this finding, we propose Trajectorylevel Importance-Corrected GRPO (TIC-GRPO), a new algorithm that replaces token-level importance ratios with a single trajectory-level proba bility ratio, thereby yielding an estimate of the current policy gradient while preserving the criticfree structure. Furthermore, we present the first convergence analysis for GRPO-style methods and show that TIC-GRPO converges faster than GRPO. Finally, empirical results across math reasoning and coding tasks demonstrate the superiority of TIC-GRPO.

## 1. Introduction

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) (Zhu et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2022; Greenblatt et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025; 2026a;b; Zhou et al., 2026) has become a standard technique for aligning large language models (LLMs) with desired behaviors. Among RLHF approaches, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017) is widely adopted but requires training an additional value network (critic), making it resource-intensive and difficult to scale. To address this, recent work proposed Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024), a critic-free alternative that estimates advantages through group-wise reward normalization while retaining PPO-style importance sampling with respect to an old policy. Owing to its simplicity and effectiveness, GRPO has been integrated into several open-source RLHF pipelines and has shown promise for broader deployment in decision-critical settings, including medical applications (Zhu et al., 2025b;a), verifiable reasoning tasks (Li et al., 2025), multi-agent collaboration (Zhang et al., 2026), embodied intelligence (Zeng et al., 2025b), and autonomous driving (Zeng et al., 2025a).

Despite its empirical success, the theoretical properties of GRPO remain underexplored. In particular, GRPO employs token-level importance sampling against the old policy, yet its update rule is not a direct estimator of the current policy gradient. We show that the practical GRPO update in fact corresponds to the policy gradient evaluated at the old policy $\pi _ { \mathrm { o l d } } .$ , plus a bias term induced by the mismatch between π and $\pi _ { \mathrm { o l d } } .$ . This bias is typically small in practice because $\pi _ { \mathrm { o l d } }$ is refreshed to the current policy every few optimization steps (e.g., every 4–10), thereby limiting divergence. An ablation study confirms this intuition: when we entirely remove importance sampling and, within each inner loop, perform all updates using gradients estimated at $\pi _ { \mathrm { o l d } }$ before refreshing it, the resulting performance remains comparable to that of standard GRPO.

Motivated by this, we propose TIC-GRPO. It replaces token-level importance weights with a single trajectorylevel ratio, and adds a lightweight stabilization patch—uponly clipping—to curb the upper-tail variance of importance weights and improve training robustness. Our theory provides the first convergence analysis for GRPO-style methods and rigorously shows that TIC-GRPO converges faster than GRPO. Finally, we validate TIC-GRPO on the standard alignment benchmark AIME; experiments show that TIC-GRPO significantly outperforms GRPO in both accuracy

and convergence rate.

Contributions This paper makes the following key contributions:

• We propose a new algorithm, TIC-GRPO, which replaces token-level importance sampling with a trajectory-level importance ratio and adds an up-only clipping stability patch to curb upper-tail variance and stabilize optimization.

• We provide the first theoretical convergence analysis for GRPO-style methods. Our results cover the original GRPO, our proposed TIC-GRPO, and an intermediate variant $\mathrm { G R P O _ { 2 } }$ that applies the token-level up-only clipping in isolation. The proven rates exhibit a clear hierarchy—from GRPO to $\mathrm { G R P O _ { 2 } }$ to TIC-GRPO— showing that each of our two modifications yields an independent convergence improvement.

• We empirically evaluate TIC-GRPO across multiple benchmarks and observe consistent improvements over recent strong baselines in both accuracy and convergence rate. Ablation results further show that both proposed modifications individually lead to performance gains, while their combination in TIC-GRPO achieves the best overall results.

Related Work A recent concurrent work by Zheng et al. (2025) proposes a similar idea of replacing token-level importance sampling in GRPO with a trajectory-level formulation, named Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO). Importantly, their work was developed independently and concurrently with ours.

In comparison, we briefly discuss why GRPO can still perform well in practice despite its inherent bias, and we provide a rigorous convergence analysis showing that TIC-GRPO attains a faster convergence rate than the original GRPO. Our implementation also differs from GSPO in two key respects: we do not apply the T-th-root (sequencelength) scaling to the trajectory-level importance ratio, and we adopt an upper-only clipping rule to control the uppertail variance of importance weights. Empirically, we include GSPO as a baseline (Section 5 and Appendix C) and observe that TIC-GRPO also achieves stronger performance.

Another important baseline considered in this work is the Decoupled Clip and Dynamic Sampling Policy Optimization (DAPO) algorithm (Yu et al., 2025).

## 2. Preliminaries and Theoretical Analysis of GRPO

## 2.1. Reinforcement Learning Setup

Let c denote the initial prompt. At each time step t, the large language model generates a token $a _ { t } \in \mathcal V .$ , where V denotes the vocabulary. Each token in V is represented as a vector in $\mathbb { R } ^ { d }$ . Then we define the state at time t as $\boldsymbol { s } _ { t } ^ { \prime } : = ( c , a _ { 1 } , \ldots , a _ { t } ) ^ { \top } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { t \times d }$ . To ensure consistent dimensionality across time steps, we embed each state into a fixed-dimensional space $\mathbb { R } ^ { T \times d }$ via zero-padding:

$$
s _ {t} := (c, a _ {1}, \ldots , a _ {t}, \underbrace {0 , \ldots , 0} _ {(T - t) \text {   tokens }}) ^ {\top},
$$

where the final $T - t$ entries are zero vectors in $\mathbb { R } ^ { d }$ . We also let $S _ { t }$ denote the set of all possible states $s _ { t } .$ . We readily observe the following inclusion relation: $\mathcal { S } _ { 1 } \subset \mathcal { S } _ { 2 } \subset . . . \subset$ $\boldsymbol { S _ { T } }$

In the LLM fine-tuning setting, it is common to work with a predefined reward function $r ( s ) : \mathbb { R } ^ { T \times d }  \mathbb { R }$ , which evaluates the quality of a complete generated sequence/state s. In many practical RLHF-style pipelines, the reward is sparse and is only provided at the terminal step $t = T$ , i.e., it depends solely on $s _ { T } ;$ moreover, the reward is typically uniformly bounded (e.g., via normalization and/or clipping), so that

$$
| r (s _ {T}) | \leq R, \quad \forall s _ {T} \in \mathcal {S} _ {T}.
$$

See, e.g., (Zheng et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Suk & Duan, 2025; Shao et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025a).

The core of an LLM is the parameterized policy. We write $\pi _ { \boldsymbol { \theta } } ( a \mid s ) : \mathbb { R } ^ { l } \times \mathbb { R } ^ { d } \times \mathbb { R } ^ { T \times d } \to [ 0 , 1 ]$ to denote the probability of generating a token $a ~ \in ~ \mathbb { R } ^ { d }$ given the current state $s \in \mathbb { R } ^ { T \times d }$ under model parameters $\theta \in \mathbb { R } ^ { l }$ . Since the token $a _ { t }$ output by the model at time step t together with the previous state $s _ { t - 1 }$ uniquely determines the state $s _ { t } ,$ we have the identity $\mathbb { P } _ { \theta } ( s _ { t } \mid s _ { t - 1 } ) = \pi _ { \theta } ( a _ { t } \mid s _ { t - 1 } )$ Here, $\mathbb P _ { \theta } \left( s _ { t } \ | \ s _ { t - 1 } \right) : \mathbb R ^ { l } \times \mathbb R ^ { T \times d } \times \mathbb R ^ { T \times d } \to \left[ 0 , 1 \right]$ ] denotes the conditional probability of the current state $s _ { t }$ given the previous state $s _ { t - 1 }$ , under parameters $\theta \in \mathbb { R } ^ { l }$

We now define the trajectory probability and value function. The joint probability of generating a full trajectory under policy π<sub>θ</sub> is given by:

$$
\mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {T} \mid c) = \prod_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1}).
$$

The goal is to maximize the expected return:

$$
\begin{array}{l} J (\theta) := \mathbb {E} _ {c \sim p (c)} \Big [ \mathbb {E} _ {s _ {T} \sim \pi_ {\theta} (\cdot | c)} \big [ r (s _ {T}) \big ] \Big ] \\ = \sum_ {c \in C} p (c) \sum_ {s _ {T} \in \mathcal {S} _ {T}} \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {T} \mid c)   r (s _ {T}). \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

Here, $p ( c )$ denotes the distribution over initial prompts, and C denotes the set of all possible prompts. Because the reward of any meaningful reinforcement learning problem is necessarily bounded, the value function $J ( \theta ) , \ ( \theta \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d } )$ admits a theoretical maximum, which we denote by $J ^ { * }$

The optimization of $J ( \theta )$ typically follows a gradient ascent (GA) scheme (Yuan et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020)<sup>1</sup>:

$$
\theta_ {n + 1} = \theta_ {n} + \eta \nabla_ {\theta_ {n}} J (\theta_ {n}),
$$

with learning rate η. Algorithms like PPO and GRPO build on this principle with various modifications to improve performance.

In LLM setting, since the reward $r ( s _ { T } )$ is assigned only at the final timestep and does not depend on $\theta ,$ the policy gradient simplifies as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \nabla J (\theta) \\ = \sum_ {c \in C} p (c) \sum_ {s _ {T} \in \mathcal {S} _ {T}} \left(\nabla \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {T} | c)\right) r (s _ {T}) \\ = \mathbb {E} _ {c \sim p (c)} \Big [ \mathbb {E} _ {s _ {T} \sim \pi_ {\theta}} [ (\nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {T} | c))   r (s _ {T}) ] \Big ]. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Notation. Throughout the remainder ofthe paper, $\nabla$ denotes gradients with respect to θ, unless explicitly stated otherwise.

## 2.2. Review of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), recently proposed by DeepSeek, is a reinforcement learning algorithm for aligning LLMs without a value-function critic. Instead of computing global advantage estimates, GRPO uses relative rewards within a group of candidate responses to estimate local advantage. Like PPO, GRPO employs a decoupled optimization structure: the old policy $\pi _ { \theta _ { \mathrm { o l d } } }$ is held fixed while the current policy $\pi _ { \theta }$ is updated over multiple gradient steps using the same batch of trajectories, improving sample efficiency.

For notational convenience, we define the σ-algebra generated by $\theta _ { \mathrm { o l d } }$ as $\mathcal { F } _ { \mathrm { o l d } } : = \sigma ( \theta _ { \mathrm { o l d } } )$

In GRPO, we first sample a set of prompts M from the distribution $p ( c )$ . For any given prompt $c \in M .$ , we generate a group of responses $G _ { c } ,$ all of which are conditioned on the same prompt c.

For notational convenience, we define, for each c, a finitely supported discrete empirical probability measure

$$
\xi_ {c} (\cdot): \mathcal {S} _ {T} \to [ 0, 1 ],
$$

induced by the group sampling $G _ { c }$ . Specifically, if a state s<sub>T</sub> appears w times in the sample $G _ { c } ,$ , then

$$
\xi_ {c} (s _ {T}) := \frac {w}{| G _ {c} |}.\tag{3}
$$

Here, $\left| G _ { c } \right|$ denotes the number of responses associated with the prompt c. Under the GRPO setting, all prompts $c \in M$ share the same group size. Accordingly, in the sequel, we denote $| G _ { c } |$ uniformly by $| G |$

With this empirical measure $\xi _ { c } ,$ the summation over the group can be written in the following form:

$$
\frac {1}{| G |} \sum_ {s _ {T} \in G _ {c}} f (s _ {T}) = \sum_ {s _ {T} \in \mathcal {S} _ {T}} \xi_ {c} (s _ {T}) f (s _ {T}),
$$

where $f : S _ { T }  \mathbb { R }$ is an arbitrary test function.

GRPO then computes normalized advantages within the group as (given $c ) ^ { 2 } { \mathrm { : } }$

$$
A _ {c} (s _ {T}) = r (s _ {T}) - \mu_ {c}, \quad \mu_ {c} = \sum_ {s _ {T} \in \mathcal {S} _ {T}} \xi_ {c} (s _ {T}) r (s _ {T}),
$$

GRPO then uniformly partitions the prompt set M into K disjoint subsets

$$
M _ {1}, M _ {2}, \ldots , M _ {K}.
$$

The algorithm performs K sequential updates indexed by $k = 1 , 2 , \dots , K$ . For each update k, we define an empirical measure over the prompt set induced by the uniform partition, denoted by

$$
\zeta_ {k} (\cdot): M \to [ 0, 1 ].
$$

Specifically, if a prompt c appears w times in the subset $M _ { k } ,$ then 21

$$
\zeta_ {k} (c) := \frac {w}{| M _ {k} |}.
$$

Here, $\lvert M _ { k } \rvert$ denotes the number of prompts assigned to the k-th subset.

The objective optimized at the k-th update is

$$
\begin{array}{c} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{GRPO}} ^ {(k) ^ {\prime}} (\theta , \theta_ {\mathrm{old}}) \\ = \sum_ {c \in C} \zeta_ {k} (c) \sum_ {s _ {T} \in \mathcal {S} _ {T}} \xi_ {c} (s _ {T}) \frac {1}{| s _ {T} |} \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \mathrm{ClipMin} (s _ {t}, \theta , \theta_ {\mathrm{old}}). \end{array}
$$

Here, $| s _ { T } |$ denotes the response length, determined by the first occurrence of a stop token: if a stop token is generated before time T, the rollout terminates at that step. We further assume that, for any θ, the policy $\pi _ { \theta }$ maps any state containing a stop token deterministically to the stop token.

The clipped per-token surrogate is defined by

$$
\begin{array}{c} \operatorname{ClipMin} (s _ {t}, \theta , \theta_ {\text {old}}) \\ := \min \left\{\frac {\mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1})}{\mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {\text {old}}} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1})} A _ {c} (s _ {T}), \right. \\ \operatorname{Clip} \bigg (\frac {\mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1})}{\mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {\text {old}}} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1})}, \epsilon_ {\text {low}}, \epsilon_ {\text {high}} \bigg) A _ {c} (s _ {T}) \bigg \}, \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

where the clipping function is

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname{Clip} (x, \epsilon_ {\text {low}}, \epsilon_ {\text {high}}) \\ := \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 - \epsilon_ {\text {low}}, & x <   1 - \epsilon_ {\text {low}}, \\ x, & 1 - \epsilon_ {\text {low}} \leq x \leq 1 + \epsilon_ {\text {high}}, \\ 1 + \epsilon_ {\text {high}}, & x > 1 + \epsilon_ {\text {high}}. \end{array} \right. \end{array}
$$

The corresponding loss function with double clipping is given by

$$
\begin{array}{c} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{GRPO}} ^ {(k)} (\theta , \theta_ {\mathrm{old}}) = \sum_ {c \in C} \zeta_ {k} (c) \sum_ {s _ {T} \in \mathcal {S} _ {T}} \xi_ {c} (s _ {T}) \frac {1}{| s _ {T} |} \\ \cdot \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \operatorname{ClipMin} _ {\text {dual}} (s _ {t}, \theta , \theta_ {\text {old}}). \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

Each objective (Eq. 5) can be optimized using gradient ascent (GA) or adaptive methods such as Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2014; Wang et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2024); in this paper, we adopt vanilla GA:

$$
\theta_ {k + 1} = \theta_ {k} + \eta \nabla \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{GRPO}} ^ {(k)} (\theta_ {k}, \theta_ {\mathrm{old}}),\tag{6}
$$

where $\eta > 0$ denotes the learning rate. After performing K gradient steps under a fixed old policy $\pi _ { \theta _ { \mathrm { o l d } } }$ , the reference is updated according to $\pi _ { \theta _ { \mathrm { o l d } } }  \pi _ { \theta }$ . The full algorithm is summarized in Eq. 10.

In original GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), the clipping thresholds are symmetric, $\mathbf { i . e . , \epsilon . } \epsilon _ { \mathrm { l o w } } = \epsilon _ { \mathrm { h i g h } }$ . A later work showed that asymmetric clipping $( \epsilon _ { \mathrm { l o w } } \neq \epsilon _ { \mathrm { h i g h } } )$ can improve performance, and termed the resulting variant Decoupled Clip and Dynamic Sampling Policy Optimization (DAPO) (Yu et al., 2025). Moreover, GRPO also includes a KL regularizer to a reference policy $\pi _ { \mathrm { r e f } } , \mathrm { K L } ( \pi _ { \theta } \parallel \pi _ { \mathrm { r e f } } )$ ; DAPO argues that after RLHF the policy may drift far from pretraining, so this KL term can be restrictive and is therefore removed (Yu et al., 2025). In this work we follow the DAPO setting and omit the KL regularization. For simplicity, we do not distinguish between the names DAPO and GRPO in what follows, since they share the same core mechanism.

## 2.3. A Decomposition of GRPO’s Gradient Term

In this section, we analyze the Gradient Term in Eq. 6 and show that it can be interpreted as an estimator of the policy gradient evaluated at $\pi _ { \theta _ { \mathrm { o l d } } }$

We now decompose the gradient of the k-th GRPO objective into a leading policy gradient term and a collection of correction terms that arise from clipping, policy mismatch, and length normalization. In particular, we obtain

$$
\begin{array}{l} \nabla \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{GRPO}} ^ {(k)} (\theta_ {k}, \theta_ {\text {old}}) \\ = \frac {1}{\mathcal {T} _ {\theta_ {\text {old}}}} \widehat {\nabla} J (\theta_ {\text {old}}) + X _ {1} (\theta_ {k}, \theta_ {\text {old}}) + X _ {2} (\theta_ {\text {old}}) + X _ {3} (\theta_ {\text {old}}) \\ + X _ {4} (\theta_ {k}, \theta_ {\text {old}}), \end{array} \tag {7}
$$

where $\widehat { \nabla } J ( \theta _ { \mathrm { o l d } } )$ denotes the empirical policy gradient estimator evaluated at the reference policy, and $X _ { 1 } { - } X _ { 4 }$ collect higher-order and clipping-induced correction terms.

Here,

$$
\mathcal {T} _ {\theta} := \mathbb {E} _ {c \sim p (c)} \left[ \mathbb {E} _ {s _ {T} \sim \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (\cdot | c)} [ | s _ {T} | ] \right]
$$

is the expected response length under the policy $\pi _ { \theta }$ , which serves as a normalization factor accounting for variablelength trajectories. See Appendix A.1for the definitions of $\widehat { \nabla } \bar { J } ( \theta _ { o l d } )$ and $X _ { 1 } ( \theta _ { k } , \theta _ { o l d } ) – X _ { 4 } ( \theta _ { k } , \theta _ { o l d } )$ , as well as a proof that $\widehat { \nabla } J ( \theta _ { o l d } )$ is an unbiased estimator of $\nabla J ( \theta _ { o l d } )$

This decomposition naturally raises the following question:

Given that GRPO evaluates a gradient estimator at the stale reference policy $\theta _ { o l d ; }$ , rather than at the current iterate $\theta _ { k } ,$ why does the algorithm nevertheless exhibit stable and effective optimization behavior in practice?

The key point is that $\pi _ { \theta _ { \mathrm { o l d } } }$ is refreshed every K steps, so π<sub>θ</sub> stays close to $\pi _ { \theta _ { \mathrm { o l d } } }$ and stale gradients remain reliable. We validate this via an ablation that removes importance sampling and, within each inner loop with fixed $\boldsymbol { \pi } _ { \boldsymbol { \theta } _ { \mathrm { o l d } } } ,$ , updates θ using policy gradients estimated under $\pi _ { \theta _ { \mathrm { o l d } } } .$ , isolating the role of importance sampling.

As shown in Figure 1, removing importance sampling causes no noticeable performance drop; in later stages it even yields a slight gain, supporting our claim that the limited drift between π<sub>θ</sub> and $\pi _ { \theta _ { \mathrm { o l d } } }$ makes $\nabla J ( \theta _ { \mathrm { o l d } } )$ a reliable update direction in practice. This motivates correcting GRPO’s importance sampling to consistently estimate the current gradient $\nabla J ( \theta )$ , and a natural fix is to replace token-level weights with a single trajectory-level importance ratio, which aligns the estimator with the true policy gradient and underlies our next-section algorithm.

## 3. Trajectory-level Importance-Corrected GRPO (TIC-GRPO)

In this section, we introduce TIC-GRPO, which improves GRPO through two complementary modifications: (i) replacing token-level importance sampling with a trajectorylevel importance ratio to correct gradient bias, and (ii) adding a lightweight stabilization patch—up-only clipping— to cap large importance weights and reduce upper-tail variance. The following discussion details these two modifications.

Trajectory-level Importance Sampling. We replace the token-level importance sampling mechanism in Eq. 5 with a trajectory-level probability ratio

$$
\frac {\mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {T} \mid c)}{\mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {\text { old }}} (s _ {T} \mid c)}.
$$

This modification enables a valid estimation of the policy gradient at the current policy.

Up-Only Clipping Mechanism. Although Eq. 4 uses standard PPO clipping, it can fail to control large importance ratios when the advantage is negative. Let

$$
r _ {t} := \frac {\mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1})}{\mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {\mathrm{old}}} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1})}.
$$

If $A _ { c } ( s _ { T } ) < 0$ and $r _ { t } > 1 + \epsilon _ { \mathrm { h i g h } }$ , then the minimum in Eq. 4 selects the unclipped term $r _ { t } A _ { c } ( s _ { T } )$ , so rare large-r<sub>t</sub> samples can dominate the update and inflate variance.

Practical systems often adopt dual clipping (Yu et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2020), i.e., two-sided clipping in the negative-advantage regime, but this typically requires a loose safeguard and may still allow large ratios to leak through. We instead use up-only clipping—a small stability patch that uniformly truncates the upper tail, especially to reduce variance when $A _ { c } ( s _ { T } ) < 0 $ :

$$
\begin{array}{l} \overline {{\text { ClipMin }}} (s _ {T}, \theta , \theta_ {\text { old }}) \\ := \min \bigg \{\frac {\mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {T} \mid c)}{\mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {\text { old }}} (s _ {T} \mid c)},   1 + \epsilon_ {\text { high }} \bigg \}   A _ {c} (s _ {T}). \end{array}
$$

We also replace the per-response normalization $1 / | s _ { T } |$ by the constant $1 / T$ to remove length-induced bias (cf. (Yu et al., 2025)), but we do not view this as a core contribution; our main algorithmic contributions are trajectory-level importance sampling and up-only clipping. Ablations show that applying up-only clipping and the $1 / T$ normalization even on token-level GRPO is already beneficial (see Subsection 4.2 and Appendix C.3).

## 3.1. Rules for TIC-GRPO

Apart from the above modifications, all other components remain consistent with the original GRPO formulation. In particular, at the k-th update step $( k = 1 , 2 , \ldots , K )$ , we optimize the following objective:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{TIC-GRPO}} ^ {(k)} (\theta , \theta_ {\mathrm{old}}) \\ = \frac {1}{T} \sum_ {c \in C} \zeta_ {k} (c) \sum_ {s _ {T} \in \mathcal {S} _ {T}} \xi_ {c} (s _ {T}) \overline {{\mathrm{ClipMin}}} (s _ {T}, \theta , \theta_ {\mathrm{old}}), \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

Similarly, we present the update rule under a fixed old policy $\pi _ { \mathrm { o l d } } :$

$$
\theta_ {k + 1} = \theta_ {k} + \eta \nabla \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{TIC-GRPO}} ^ {(k)} (\theta_ {k}, \theta_ {\mathrm{old}}),
$$

where $\eta$ is the learning rate. As in GRPO, the old policy $\pi _ { \theta _ { \mathrm { o l d } } }$ is refreshed every K steps by assigning $\pi _ { \theta _ { \mathrm { o l d } } }  \pi _ { \theta }$ The complete algorithm is summarized in Eq. 11

## 3.2. A Decomposition of GRPO’s Gradient Term

The gradient of the TIC-GRPO objective at the k-th update admits the following decomposition:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \nabla \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{TIC-GRPO}} ^ {(k)} (\theta_ {k}, \theta_ {\mathrm{old}}) \\ = \frac {1}{T} \widetilde {\nabla} J (\theta_ {k}) + Y _ {1} (\theta_ {k}, \theta_ {\mathrm{old}}) + Y _ {2} (\theta_ {k}, \theta_ {\mathrm{old}}). \end{array}\tag{9}
$$

Here, the leading term $\widetilde { \nabla } J ( \theta _ { k } )$ corresponds to a policy gradient estimator evaluated at the current iterate $\theta _ { k }$ . We defer the explicit forms of $\widetilde { \nabla } J ( \theta _ { k } ) , Y _ { 1 }$ , and $Y _ { 2 }$ to Appendix A.2, where we also prove the (un)biasedness o $f \widetilde { \nabla } J ( \theta _ { k } )$

This contrasts with standard GRPO, where the surrogate gradient is typically anchored at $\theta _ { \mathrm { o l d } }$ rather than $\theta _ { k }$ . ## 4. Convergence Results

In this section, we derive stationary-point sample complexity guarantees for GRPO and TIC-GRPO, and we also analyze the intermediate variant that separately adds the two modifications in Subsection 4.2 to GRPO’s token-level mechanism.

To facilitate convergence analysis, we begin by rewriting both algorithms in iterative update forms:

GRPO

$$
\begin{array}{l} \theta_ {n, 0} = \theta_ {n - 1, K}, \\ \theta_ {n, k + 1} = \theta_ {n, k} + \eta \widehat {\nabla} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{GRPO}} (\theta_ {n, k}, \theta_ {n, 0}). \end{array}\tag{10}
$$

TIC-GRPO

$$
\begin{array}{l} \theta_ {n, 0} = \theta_ {n - 1, K}, \\ \theta_ {n, k + 1} = \theta_ {n, k} + \eta \widehat {\nabla} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{TIC-GRPO}} (\theta_ {n, k}, \theta_ {n, 0}). \end{array}\tag{11}
$$

Here n indexes outer iterations (each refresh of $\pi _ { \mathrm { o l d } } )$ , and $k = 0 , 1 , \ldots , K$ indexes inner updates using disjoint minibatches from the dataset collected under $\pi _ { \mathrm { o l d } }$ . In particular, $\theta _ { n , 0 } = \theta _ { n - 1 , K }$

For subsequent stochastic-process analysis, we introduce the natural outer–inner filtrations. Define the outer filtration $\mathcal { F } _ { n } : = \sigma ( \theta _ { 1 , 0 } , . . . , \theta _ { n , 0 } )$ , and, for the inner loop at outer index n, define $\mathcal { F } _ { n , k } : = \sigma ( \mathcal { F } _ { n } , \theta _ { n , 1 } , . . . , \theta _ { n , k } )$ k = $0 , 1 , \ldots , K .$ . In particular, $\begin{array} { r } { \mathcal { \vec { F } } _ { n , 0 } = \mathcal { F } _ { n - 1 , K } : = \mathcal { F } _ { n } . } \end{array}$

We now present the smoothness assumption that underlie our convergence analysis for both GRPO and TIC-GRPO.

Assumption 4.1 (Lipschitz score in θ). There exists $L > 0$ such thatfor all $( s _ { t } , s _ { t - 1 } ) \in S _ { T } \times S _ { T }$ and all $\theta , \theta ^ { \prime }$

$$
\left\| \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1}) - \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta^ {\prime}} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1}) \right\| \leq L \| \theta - \theta^ {\prime} \|.
$$

This is a standard and widely accepted assumption in the theoretical analysis of policy gradient methods, including PPO- and GRPO-type algorithms, and it has been adopted in a number of recent works (Zhang et al., 2023; Xiong et al., 2021; Ding et al., 2021; Bedi et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025b; Suk & Duan, 2025). Moreover, unlike Xiong et al. (2021); Liu et al. (2025b); Suk & Duan (2025), we do not impose boundedness of the score function itself. By avoiding this additional restriction, our theory applies to a broader class of policies.

## 4.1. Results of GRPO

We now present the convergence result for the original GRPO:

Theorem 4.1. (Convergence of GRPO) Assume that the conditions stated in Assumptions 4.1 is satisfied. Let $\theta _ { 1 , 0 } \in$ $\mathbb { R } ^ { d }$ denote an arbitrary initialization of the algorithm, and we set $\begin{array} { r } { \eta = \frac { 1 } { \sqrt { N } \log | \mathcal { V } | } } \end{array}$ . Then the sequence $\{ \theta _ { n , k } \}$ generated by GRPO as defined in Eq. 10 admits the following upper bound:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbb {E} \left[ \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N - 1} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {K - 1} \| \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) \| ^ {2} \right] \\ \leq \mathcal {O} \left(\frac {T ^ {7 / 2} \log | \mathcal {V} |}{\sqrt {N}} \cdot \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N - 1} \mathcal {M} _ {n} ^ {2} \sigma_ {\theta_ {n, 0}} ^ {2}\right) \\ + \mathcal {O} \left(\frac {T ^ {2} \log | \mathcal {V} |}{| G |}\right). \end{array}
$$

Here ${ \cal S } _ { T } ^ { ( n ) }$ denotes the set ofall trajectories sampled under $\pi _ { \theta _ { n , 0 } }$ at the n-th outer iteration.

$$
\mathcal {M} _ {n} := \max _ {s _ {T} \in \mathcal {S} _ {T} ^ {(n)}} \frac {1}{\mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {n , 0}} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1})}.\tag{12}
$$

Moreover, $\sigma _ { \theta _ { n , 0 } } ^ { 2 }$ denotes the variance of response lengths over trajectories sampled at outer iteration n under $\pi _ { \boldsymbol { \theta } _ { n , 0 } } .$

$$
\sigma_ {\theta_ {n, 0}} ^ {2} :=\tag{13}
$$

$$
\mathbb {E} _ {c \sim p (c)} \mathbb {E} _ {s _ {T} \sim \mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {n, 0}} (\cdot | c)} \left[ \left(\left| s _ {T} \right| - \mathbb {E} _ {s _ {T} \sim \mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {n, 0}} (\cdot | c)} \left[ \left| s _ {T} \right| \right]\right) ^ {2} \right].
$$

Due to space constraints, the proof is deferred to Section D. This is the first rigorous convergence guarantee for GRPO. The rate depends on two non-optimizable quantities, $\mathcal { M } _ { n }$ and $\sigma _ { \theta _ { n , 0 } }$ . The $\mathcal { M } _ { n }$ term stems from the fact that the conventional clipping only truncates one side of the importance ratio (when the advantage is negative), leaving the other side uncontrolled; hence the variance can only be bounded via $\mathcal { M } _ { n }$ . The $\sigma _ { \theta _ { n , 0 } }$ term is caused by variable response lengths under the same prompt, while GRPO applies per-response length normalization, inducing an additional fixed error. The last term $\mathcal { O } \left( \frac { T ^ { 2 } \log | \mathcal { V } | } { | G | } \right)$ is an intrinsic bias from using the within-group mean advantage (a surrogate value), as also noted by (Yang et al., 2026); it becomes negligible for large |G|.

Importantly, removing the dependence on $\mathcal { M } _ { n }$ and $\sigma _ { \theta _ { n , 0 } }$ is easy algorithmically: (i) use the up-only clipping in Section 3 to eliminate $\mathcal { M } _ { n }$ , and (ii) replace the length regularization by a uniform $1 / T$ normalization to remove $\sigma _ { \theta _ { n , 0 } } $ . This does not require trajectory-level importance sampling; in the next subsection we introduce an intermediate algorithm that isolates the effects of up-only clipping and uniform length normalization.

## 4.2. Isolating the Effect of Length Normalization and Up-Only Clipping

To isolate the independent roles of up-only clipping and the uniform length regularization, we introduce an intermediate algorithm, termed $\mathrm { G R P O _ { 2 } }$ , whose procedure is given below. At the k-th inner update, $\mathrm { G R P O _ { 2 } }$ maximizes

$$
\begin{array}{c} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{GRPO} _ {2}} ^ {(k)} (\theta , \theta_ {\mathrm{old}}) \\ = \frac {1}{T} \sum_ {c \in C} \zeta_ {k} (c) \sum_ {s _ {T} \in \mathcal {S} _ {T}} \xi_ {c} (s _ {T}) \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \overline {{\mathrm{ClipMin}}} (s _ {t}, \theta , \theta_ {\mathrm{old}}), \end{array}\tag{14}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{l} \overline {{\text {ClipMin}}} (s _ {t}, \theta , \theta_ {\text {old}}) \\ := \min \biggl \{\frac {\mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1})}{\mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {\text {old}}} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1})}, 1 + \epsilon_ {\text {high}} \biggr \} A _ {c} (s _ {T}). \end{array}
$$

Similarly, $\mathrm { G R P O _ { 2 } }$ admits a decomposition analogous to those for GRPO and TIC-GRPO. For the gradient decomposition $o f \mathrm { G R P O _ { 2 } }$ and additional implementation details, see Appendix A.3.

We are now ready to state the convergence guarantee for $\mathrm { G R P O _ { 2 } }$ . Moreover, since $\mathrm { G R P O _ { 2 } }$ adopts the uniform $1 / T$ normalization, its convergence rate exhibits a substantially improved dependence on T compared with the original GRPO.

Theorem 4.2 (Convergence of $\mathrm { G R P O _ { 2 } ) }$ . Assume Assumptions 4.1 holds. Let $\{ \theta _ { n , k } \}$ be generated by $G R P O _ { 2 }$ with

$$
\theta_ {n, k + 1} = \theta_ {n, k} + \eta \nabla \mathcal {L} _ {G R P O _ {2}} ^ {(k)} (\theta_ {n, k}, \theta_ {n, 0}),\tag{15}
$$

and step size $\begin{array} { r } { \eta = \frac { 1 } { \sqrt { N } \log | \mathcal { V } | } } \end{array}$ . Then,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {K - 1} \mathbb {E} \big [ \| \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) \| ^ {2} \big ] \\ \leq \mathcal {O} \left(\frac {T ^ {5 / 2} \log | \mathcal {V} |}{\sqrt {N}}\right) + \mathcal {O} \left(\frac {T ^ {2} \log | \mathcal {V} |}{| G |}\right). \end{array}\tag{16}
$$

Due to space constraints, the proof is deferred to Section D. It can be seen that, compared with the original GRPO, $\mathrm { G R P O _ { 2 ^ { - } } }$ —which only incorporates upp-only clipping and the uniform length regularization—achieves a strictly improved convergence rate, since it no longer depends on $\mathcal { M } _ { n }$ or $\sigma _ { \theta _ { n , 0 } }$

In the next subsection, we further present the convergence rate of TIC-GRPO, which builds on $\mathrm { G R P O _ { 2 } }$ and additionally improves the dependence on T.

## 4.3. Results of TIC-GRPO

We next present the convergence guarantee for TIC-GRPO. Theorem 4.3 (Convergence of TIC-GRPO). Assume that the conditions stated in Assumptions 4.1 holds. Let $\theta _ { 1 , 0 } \in$ $\mathbb { R } ^ { d }$ denote an arbitrary initialization of the algorithm, and we set $\begin{array} { r } { \eta = \frac { 1 } { \sqrt { N } \log | \mathcal { V } | } } \end{array}$ . Then the sequence $\{ \theta _ { n , k } \}$ generated by TIC-GRPO as defined in Eq. 11 admits the following upper bound:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N - 1} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {K - 1} \mathbb {E} \big [ \| \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) \| ^ {2} \big ] \\ \leq \mathcal {O} \left(\frac {T \log | \mathcal {V} |}{\sqrt {N}}\right) + \mathcal {O} \left(\frac {T \log | \mathcal {V} |}{| G |}\right). \end{array}
$$

Due to space constraints, the proof is deferred to Section D.

It can be seen that, compared with $\mathrm { G R P O _ { 2 } }$ , TIC-GRPO further improves the dependence on T in the convergence rate. This highlights the standalone effect of trajectorylevel importance sampling. Moreover, Theorems 4.1–4.3 reveal a clear, tiered improvement in the convergence rates, which provides theoretical evidence that both modifications proposed in this paper are indeed effective. In Appendix C.3, we further corroborate this claim via ablation studies.

Next, we explain a key reason why TIC-GRPO achieves a better T-dependence than $\mathrm { G R P O _ { 2 } } \mathrm { ; }$ it fully preserves the latent martingale-difference structure of the sequence score function.

## 4.4. Latent martingale-difference structure

Fix a prompt c and parameter θ. For a trajectory $s _ { T } =$ $( s _ { 1 } , \ldots , s _ { T } )$ with $s _ { 0 } : = c ,$ the Markov factorization gives

$$
\nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {T} \mid c) = \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1}).
$$

Let $\mathcal { G } _ { t } : = \sigma ( s _ { 0 } , \ldots , s _ { t } )$ . Then for each $t \geq 1$

$$
\mathbb {E} \big [ \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1}) \big | \mathcal {G} _ {t - 1} \big ] = 0,
$$

so the one-step score terms form a martingale-difference sequence. This yields the following useful second-moment identity.

Property 4.1. Fix any prompt $c \in C$ and any $\theta \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d }$ . The following identity holds:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \mathbb {E} _ {s _ {T} \sim \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (\cdot | c)} \bigg [ \| \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {T} \mid c) \| ^ {2} \bigg ] \\ = \mathbb {E} _ {s _ {T} \sim \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (\cdot | c)} \left[ \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \| \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1}) \| ^ {2} \right], \end{array}
$$

where we set $s _ { 0 } : = c _ { }$

The proof of this property can be found in Appendix B.

For essentially any stochastic algorithm, the convergence rate is driven by the quadratic update error

E $\left[ \lVert { \boldsymbol { \theta } } _ { n , k + 1 } - { \boldsymbol { \theta } } _ { n , k } \rVert ^ { 2 } \right]$ . By the TIC-GRPO update, it suffices to bound

$$
Q := \mathbb {E} _ {s _ {T} \sim \mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {n, 0}} (\cdot | c)} \left[ \left\| \operatorname{clip} \left(\rho_ {0: T}\right) \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {n, k}} \left(s _ {T} \mid c\right) \right\| ^ {2} \right],
$$

where $\rho _ { 0 : T }$ denotes the trajectory-level importance ratio (shorthand). Using trajectory-level importance sampling together with clipping, we further obtain

$$
Q \leq (1 + \epsilon_ {\text {high}}) \mathbb {E} _ {s _ {T} \sim \mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {n, k}} (\cdot | c)} \left[ \left\| \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {n, k}} (s _ {T} \mid c) \right\| ^ {2} \right].
$$

This reduction crucially requires both mechanisms: $\rho _ { 0 : T }$ performs the measure change from $\mathbb { P } _ { \theta _ { n , 0 } } \tan \mathbb { P } _ { \theta _ { n , k } }$ , and clipping enforces a uniform bound on the weight. In particular, the T-th-root scaling used in GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025) is incompatible with this step, since the resulting weight is no longer the Radon–Nikodym derivative d $\mathbb { P } _ { \theta _ { n , k } } / \mathrm { d } \mathbb { P } _ { \theta _ { n , 0 } }$ and the measure-change argument fails. Finally, Property 4.1 converts the trajectory-score second moment into a sum of one-step score second moments, without introducing any additional factor of $T .$

In contrast, for $\mathrm { G R P O _ { 2 } }$ (we do not compare to GRPO directly since GRPO entails an additional, unrelated confounding effect due to incomplete clipping), the importance sampling and clipping are applied token-wise. Consequently, the relevant quadratic error involves

$$
\mathbb {E} _ {s _ {T} \sim \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (\cdot | c)} \left[ \left\| \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \mathrm{clip} (\rho_ {t})   \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1}) \right\| ^ {2} \right],
$$

where $\rho _ { t }$ is the token-level importance ratio at step t. This token-wise weighting breaks the martingale-difference structure, so Property 4.1 no longer applies; one typically resorts to mean/Cauchy–Schwarz bounds, incurring an extra dependence on T.

## 6. Conclusion

We propose TIC-GRPO, which replaces GRPO’s tokenlevel importance sampling with a trajectory-level ratio and stabilizes training via an upper-only clipping rule that avoids high variance when the advantage is negative. We provide the first rigorous convergence analysis for GRPO-type algorithms, isolating and validating both modifications, and our experiments further corroborate these findings.

## Impact Statement

This work proposes TIC-GRPO, improving GRPO-style RLHF by using trajectory-level importance correction and an upper-only clipping rule, and provides convergence analysis with empirical validation. The main positive impact is more stable and reproducible RL fine-tuning. However, more efficient RLHF can also be misused to optimize harmful objectives or amplify reward-model/data biases, so deployment should follow responsible training practices (safety evaluations, monitoring, and access controls).

Using the score identity,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbb {E} \Big [ \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {j} \mid s _ {j - 1})   \Big |   \mathcal {G} _ {j - 1} \Big ] = \mathbb {E} _ {s _ {j} \sim \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (\cdot | s _ {j - 1})} [ \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {j} \mid s _ {j - 1}) ] \\ \qquad = \sum_ {s _ {j}} \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {j} \mid s _ {j - 1})   \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {j} \mid s _ {j - 1}) = \sum_ {s _ {j}} \nabla \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {j} \mid s _ {j - 1}) \\ \qquad = \nabla \sum_ {s _ {j}} \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {j} \mid s _ {j - 1}) = \nabla 1 = 0. \end{array}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\mathbb {E} \left[ \left\langle \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {i} \mid s _ {i - 1}), \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {j} \mid s _ {j - 1}) \right\rangle \mid \mathcal {G} _ {j - 1} \right] = \left\langle \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {i} \mid s _ {i - 1}), \mathbb {E} [ \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {j} \mid s _ {j - 1}) \mid \mathcal {G} _ {j - 1} ] \right\rangle = 0.
$$

Taking expectation again implies the cross terms vanish in expectation. Hence,

$$
\mathbb {E} _ {s _ {T} \sim \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (\cdot | c)} \left[ \left\| \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {T} \mid c) \right\| ^ {2} \right] = \mathbb {E} _ {s _ {T} \sim \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (\cdot | c)} \left[ \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \left\| \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta} (s _ {t} \mid s _ {t - 1}) \right\| ^ {2} \right],
$$

which is exactly the claimed identity.

## C.3. Ablation Study

To isolate the contribution of each design component in TIC-GRPO, as introduced in Section 3, we conduct an ablation study on the Qwen3-1.7B model, with GRPO serving as the baseline. Specifically, we evaluate the following variants:

• GRPO+Traj IS: replaces the token-level importance sampling used in GRPO with the Trajectory-level Importance Sampling, while keeping the original optimization procedure unchanged;

• GRPO+UB Clip: applies the proposed Up-Only Clipping Mechanism, together with replacing the per-response normalization by a constant factor $1 / T ;$

• TIC-GRPO: combines both Trajectory-level Importance Sampling and the Up-Only Clipping Mechanism.

As shown in Table 2 and Figure 4, both individual modifications consistently improve performance over the GRPO baseline across benchmarks, indicating that trajectory-level importance correction and upper-bound clipping each contribute positively to optimization. In particular, GRPO+Traj IS yields clear and stable gains across tasks, demonstrating the benefit of correcting importance weights at the trajectory level. Meanwhile, GRPO+UB Clip achieves more pronounced improvements, especially in terms of reward growth and convergence behavior, highlighting the importance of controlling the upper-tail variance of importance weights. When combined, TIC-GRPO consistently attains the strongest overall performance, validating the complementary effects of the two refinements.

\begin{array}{l} \sum_ {c \in C} p (c) \sum_ {s _ {T} \in \mathcal {S} _ {T}} \mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {n, k}} (s _ {T} | c) \left\| \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {n, k}} (s _ {T} | c) \right\| ^ {2} \\ = \mathbb {E} _ {c \sim p (c)} \left[ \mathbb {E} _ {s _ {T} \sim \mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {n, k}} (\cdot | c)} \left[ \left\| \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {n, k}} (s _ {T} | c) \right\| ^ {2} \right] \right] \\ \stackrel {{\text {Lemma 4.1}}} {{=}} \mathbb {E} _ {c \sim p (c)} \left[ \mathbb {E} _ {s _ {T} \sim \mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {n, k}} (\cdot | c)} \left[ \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \left\| \nabla \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {n, k}} (s _ {t} | s _ {t - 1}) \right\| ^ {2} \right] \right] \\ \leq^ {\text {Lemma D.15}} \leq \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \mathbb {E} _ {c \sim p (c)} \left[ \mathbb {E} _ {s _ {T} \sim \mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {n, k}} (\cdot | c)} \left[ - 2 L \log \mathbb {P} _ {\theta_ {n, k}} (s _ {t} | s _ {t - 1}) \right] \right] \\ \stackrel {{J e n s e n ^ {\prime} s}} {{\leq}} 2 T L \log | \mathcal {V} |. \end{array}
$$

Substituting the above result into Eq. 46 yields

$$
\mathbb {E} \left[ \left\| \theta_ {n, k + 1} - \theta_ {n, k} \right\| ^ {2} \mid \mathcal {F} _ {t - 1} \right] \leq \frac {2 \eta^ {2} L \log | \mathcal {V} |}{T}.
$$

With this, we complete the proof.

□

## E.4.4. THE PROOF OF THEOREM 4.3

Proof. First, we compute the difference in the value function induced by two adjacent inner-loop updates,

$$
\big (J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {n, k + 1}) \big) - \big (J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {n, k}) \big).
$$

Specifically, we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} \left(J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {n, k + 1})\right) - \left(J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {n, k})\right) \\ \stackrel {{\text {Lemma D.1}}} {{\leq}} - \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) ^ {\top} (\theta_ {n, k + 1} - \theta_ {n, k}) + \frac {T R L}{2} (2 \log | \mathcal {V} | + 1) \| \theta_ {n, k + 1} - \theta_ {n, k} \| ^ {2} \\ \leq - \eta \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) ^ {\top} \nabla \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{TIC-GRPO}} ^ {k} (\theta_ {n, k}, \theta_ {n, 0}) + \frac {T R L}{2} (2 \log | \mathcal {V} | + 1) \| \theta_ {n, k + 1} - \theta_ {n, k} \| ^ {2}. \end{array}
$$

Next, we substitute the decomposition of $\nabla \mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { T I C - G R P O } } ^ { k } ( \theta _ { n , k } , \theta _ { n , 0 } )$ shown in Eq. 9 into the above expression, to obtain

$$
\begin{array}{l} \big (J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {n, k}) \big) - \big (J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {n, k + 1}) \big) \\ \leq - \frac {\eta}{T} \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) ^ {\top} \widetilde {\nabla} J (\theta_ {n, k}) + \eta   \| \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) \|   \| Y _ {1} (\theta_ {n, k}, \theta_ {n, 0}) \| + \eta   \| \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) \|   \| Y _ {2} (\theta_ {n, k}, \theta_ {n, 0}) \| \\ + \frac {T R L}{2} (2 \log | \mathcal {V} | + 1) \| \theta_ {n, k + 1} - \theta_ {n, k} \| ^ {2}. \end{array}
$$

Next, we take the conditional expectation with respect to $\mathcal { F } _ { n }$ on both sides of the above inequality, to obtain:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\mathbb {E} \left[ \left(J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {n, k + 1})\right) | \mathcal {F} _ {n} \right] - \left(J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {n, k})\right)} \\ & {\leq - \frac {\eta}{2 T} \mathbb {E} \left[ \| \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) \| ^ {2} | \mathcal {F} _ {n} \right] + T \eta \mathbb {E} \left[ \| Y _ {1} (\theta_ {n, k}, \theta_ {n, 0}) \| ^ {2} | \mathcal {F} _ {n} \right] + T \eta \mathbb {E} \left[ \| Y _ {2} (\theta_ {n, 0}, \theta_ {n, k}) \| ^ {2} | \mathcal {F} _ {n} \right]} \\ & {\quad + T R L (2 \log | \mathcal {V} | + 1) \mathbb {E} \left[ \| \theta_ {n, k + 1} - \theta_ {n, k} \| ^ {2} | \mathcal {F} _ {n} \right].} \end{array}
$$

In the above derivation, we upper bound the cross terms using the elementary mean inequality, i.e.,

$$
\left\| \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) \right\| \left\| Y _ {i} (\theta_ {n, 0}, \theta_ {n, k}) \right\| \leq \frac {1}{4 T} \left\| \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) \right\| ^ {2} + T \left\| Y _ {i} (\theta_ {n, 0}, \theta_ {n, k}) \right\| ^ {2}, \quad (i = 1, 2).
$$

Then, substituting the results of Lemma D.11-D.13 into the above inequality, we obtain

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbb {E} \left[ \left(J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {n, k})\right) | \mathcal {F} _ {n} \right] - \left(J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {n, k + 1})\right) \\ \leq - \frac {\eta}{2 T} \mathbb {E} \left[ \| \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) \| ^ {2} | \mathcal {F} _ {n} \right] + \frac {K (1 + \epsilon_ {\mathrm{high}}) L \eta}{| G |} \log | \mathcal {V} | \\ \quad + T \eta \left(8 R ^ {2} L \log | \mathcal {V} | \left(K \frac {\sqrt {2 L \log | \mathcal {V} |}}{\log (1 + \epsilon_ {\mathrm{high}})} \frac {\sqrt {2 \eta^ {2} L \log | \mathcal {V} |}}{T} + \frac {K L}{\log (1 + \epsilon_ {\mathrm{high}})} \frac {2 \eta^ {2} L \log | \mathcal {V} |}{T}\right)\right) \\ \quad + \frac {T R L}{2} (2 \log | \mathcal {V} | + 1) \frac {2 \eta^ {2} L \log | \mathcal {V} |}{T} \\ = - \frac {\eta}{2 T} \mathbb {E} \left[ \| \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) \| ^ {2} | \mathcal {F} _ {n} \right] \\ \quad + \mathcal {O} \bigg (\frac {\eta \log | \mathcal {V} |}{| G |} + K \eta^ {2} (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} + K \eta^ {3} (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} + \eta^ {2} (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} \bigg). \end{array}
$$

Taking total expectation on both sides and using $\mathbb { E } [ \mathbb { E } [ \cdot \mid \mathcal { F } _ { n } ] ] = \mathbb { E } [ \cdot ]$ , we obtain

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbb {E} [ J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {n, k + 1}) ] - \mathbb {E} [ J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {n, k}) ] \\ \leq - \frac {\eta}{2 T} \mathbb {E} \big [ \| \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) \| ^ {2} \big ] \\ \quad + \mathcal {O} \bigg (\frac {\eta \log | \mathcal {V} |}{| G |} + K \eta^ {2} (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} + K \eta^ {3} (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} + \eta^ {2} (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} \bigg). \end{array}\tag{47}
$$

Summing Eq. 47 over $k = 0 , 1 , \ldots , K - 1$ yields the telescoping

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbb {E} [ J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {n, K}) ] - \mathbb {E} [ J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {n, 0}) ] \\ \leq - \frac {\eta}{2 T} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {K - 1} \mathbb {E} \big [ \| \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) \| ^ {2} \big ] \\ + K \cdot \mathcal {O} \bigg (\frac {\eta \log | \mathcal {V} |}{| G |} + K \eta^ {2} (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} + K \eta^ {3} (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} + \eta^ {2} (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} \bigg). \end{array}\tag{48}
$$

Using ${ \theta } _ { n , K } = { \theta } _ { n + 1 , 0 }$ , we can rewrite Eq. 48 as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbb {E} [ J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {n + 1, 0}) ] - \mathbb {E} [ J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {n, 0}) ] \\ \leq - \frac {\eta}{2 T} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {K - 1} \mathbb {E} \big [ \| \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) \| ^ {2} \big ] \\ \quad + K \cdot \mathcal {O} \bigg (\frac {\eta \log | \mathcal {V} |}{| G |} + K \eta^ {2} (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} + K \eta^ {3} (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} + \eta^ {2} (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} \bigg). \end{array}\tag{49}
$$

Finally, summing Eq. 49 over $n = 1 , 2 , \ldots , N$ yields

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbb {E} [ J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {N, 0}) ] - \mathbb {E} [ J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {1, 0}) ] \\ \leq - \frac {\eta}{2 T} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N - 1} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {K - 1} \mathbb {E} \big [ \| \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) \| ^ {2} \big ] \\ + N K \cdot \mathcal {O} \left(\frac {K \eta \log | \mathcal {V} |}{| G |} + K \eta^ {2} (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} + K \eta^ {3} (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} + \eta^ {2} (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2}\right). \end{array}\tag{50}
$$

Rearranging Eq. 50 gives

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {1}{N K} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N - 1} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {K - 1} \mathbb {E} \big [ \| \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) \| ^ {2} \big ] \\ \leq \frac {2 T}{\eta N K} \mathbb {E} [ J ^ {*} - J (\theta_ {1, 0}) ] \\ + \mathcal {O} \bigg (\frac {T \log | \mathcal {V} |}{| G |} \bigg) + T \mathcal {O} \big (K \eta (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} + K \eta^ {2} (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} + \eta (\log | \mathcal {V} |) ^ {2} \big). \end{array}\tag{51}
$$

Taking

$$
\eta = \frac {1}{\sqrt {N \log^ {2} | \mathcal {V} |}},
$$

we obtain

$$
\frac {1}{N} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N - 1} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {K - 1} \mathbb {E} \big [ \| \nabla J (\theta_ {n, k}) \| ^ {2} \big ] \leq \mathcal {O} \left(\frac {T \log | \mathcal {V} |}{\sqrt {N}}\right) + \mathcal {O} \left(\frac {T \log | \mathcal {V} |}{| G |}\right).\tag{52}
$$

With this, we complete the proof of this theorem.
