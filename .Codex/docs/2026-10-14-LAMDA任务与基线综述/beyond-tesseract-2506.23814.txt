 Reassessing ML-based Malware Detection under Spatio-Temporal Drift

 Abstract 
 
 Several recent works focused on the best practices for applying machine learning to cybersecurity. In the context of malware, Tesseract highlighted the impact of concept drift on detection performance and suggested temporal and spatial constraints to be enforced to ensure realistic time-aware evaluations, which have been adopted by the community. 
 
 In this paper, we demonstrate striking discrepancies in the performance of learning-based malware detection across the same time frame when evaluated on two representative Android malware datasets used in top-tier security conferences, both adhering to established sampling and evaluation guidelines. 
 
 This questions our ability to understand how current state-of-the-art approaches would perform in realistic scenarios. 
 
 To address this, we identify five novel temporal and spatial bias factors that affect realistic evaluations. We thoroughly evaluate the impact of these factors in the Android malware domain on two representative datasets and five Android malware classifiers used or proposed in top-tier security conferences. For each factor, we provide practical and actionable recommendations that the community should integrate in their methodology for more realistic and reproducible settings. 
 
 
 
 I Introduction 

 
 Despite more than a decade of research on machine learning for malware detection, the problem remains far from solved. For example, Tesseract [ 29 ] , inspired by other works [ 6 , 27 ] , demonstrated the impact of concept drift on malware classifiers, and provided constraints and suggestions to eliminate experimental bias and have more realistic evaluations. 
 
 
 The AndroZoo [ 5 , 7 , 1 ] project generated new hope among researchers to have more realistic, large-scale evaluations. Periodically, AndroZoo crawls applications from multiple markets (e.g., GooglePlay, AppChina), and as of today provides the largest data source of apk , with over 25 millions apps from over 15 years. When crawled, apk are submitted to the VirusTotal [ 4 ] service, which runs apps against multiple AV ( AV ) engines and produce detailed analysis reports (e.g., with family information). 
 
 
 These repositories and frameworks, such as Tesseract , advocating guidelines for evaluations in non-stationary security contexts [ 29 , 27 , 6 , 10 , 18 ] , have enabled more realistic, time-aware evaluations of ML ( ML ) methods for Android malware detection. Or have they? 
 
 
 To challenge this assumption, let us consider two state-of-the-art datasets used in top-tier security venues and sampled from AndroZoo: APIGraph [ 37 ] and Transcendent [ 12 ] .
Both datasets strictly abide by all constraints outlined in Tesseract [ 29 ] to eliminate spatial and temporal bias, and align with all best practices for dataset construction and evaluation [ 10 , 18 ] . Surprisingly, evaluation across five state-of-the-art malware detectors reveals substantial discrepancy in F 1 F_{1} -Score on the two datasets (see Figure 1 ), with detection performance on APIGraph being consistently higher than on Transcendent across the same time frame. We postulate that the spatio-temporal constraints in prior work [ 29 , 10 , 18 , 32 ] do not entirely eliminate experimental bias, and that there are other nuanced factors to consider. 
 
 
 More specifically, we identify five new spatio-temporal factors that can affect dataset sampling and evaluation of a learning-based Android malware detector.
After introducing our hypotheses, we empirically evaluate the impact of each factor using five representative state-of-the-art Android malware classifiers: Drebin [ 11 ] , DeepDrebin [ 20 ] , MalScan [ 36 ] , Ramda [ 23 ] , and Hcc [ 15 ] . Based on our findings, we provide practical recommendations for each of the five factors to reduce spatial and temporal biases, demonstrating how to practically apply our recommendations in a newly-sampled dataset in §V . 
 
 
 In summary, we make the following contributions: 
 
 • 
 
 We discover five novel spatio-temporal bias factors ( §IV ) beyond Tesseract [ 29 ] , which can lead to misleading results even if following all current recommendations for realistic evaluations [ 18 , 33 , 29 , 10 , 32 ] , as shown in Figure 1 . 
 
 • 
 
 We systematically analyze the impact of these factors on five state-of-the-art Android classifiers [ 11 , 20 , 36 , 23 , 15 ] ), and two datasets [ 37 , 12 ] .Where necessary, we augment our analysis by sampling additional data from AndroZoo to further generalize our findings outside of Transcendent and APIGraph datasets. 
 
 • 
 
 We showcase the practicality of our recommendations by performing a case study on a newly sampled dataset between 2021–2023 ( §V ), that follows our sampling recommendations and evaluation guidelines.

 Availability 

 
 To ensure reproducibility, and to encourage and foster bias-free evaluations in Android malware classification, we release the hashes and features of the dataset curated in §V at this link: https://bit.ly/hypercube_dataset . We will publicly release all the other code and artifacts upon publication of this paper. 
 
 
 
 
 II Preliminary Observations 

 
 We aim to investigate the reasons behind the striking performance difference observed in Figure 1 , regardless of the feature representation or classifier used. 
 
 
 We focus our attention on two representative Android datasets published in top-tier security conferences:
 APIGraph [ 37 ] ( D A D_{A} ) and Transcendent [ 12 ] ( D T D_{T} ).
We selected these datasets as they adhere to all spatial and temporal constraints outlined in Tesseract [ 29 ] and align with recommendations from Arp et al. [ 10 ] . Moreover, they overlap in 2014–2018; hence, we restrict D A D_{A} to this period to match the time frame of D T D_{T} , allowing us to perform direct comparison between the two datasets. 
 
 
 Family Overlap 

 
 Prior work [ 16 ] showed that the appearance of new malware families were the major cause of drift in D T D_{T} . Hence, we investigate the distribution differences between the two datasets by computing the “family overlap,” i.e., the percentage of malware families for each testing year 2015–2018 that existed during the training year 2014; a more formal definition can be found in Appendix C . Figure 2 shows that new malware families steadily replace older ones in D T D_{T} , while malware family overlap does not show a clear trend in D A D_{A} . Considering that both datasets were sampled within the same time frame, we postulate that certain factors differed during the curation of the dataset, resulting in the different distributions of malware samples. 
 
 
 Figure 2 : Family overlap of D A D_{A} and D T D_{T} at test time (2015–2018) with respect to families in the training set (2014). 
 
 
 
 Novel Spatio-Temporal Bias Factors 

 
 To identify possible sources of bias that explain the difference in performance in Figure 1 , we first examine the high-level statistics of D A D_{A} and D T D_{T} . We summarize the main differences identified in Table I . Based on these differences, we hypothesize five novel spatio-temporal bias factors that have been overlooked in previous work and may cause the observed performance gap. The App Markets affect the sampling of both goodware and malware apps, whereas higher labeling VT Thresholds may lead to malware that is “easier to detect.” While Dataset Size is similar between D A D_{A} and D T D_{T} , it needs to be investigated whether it is statistically significant and can be expected to reliably reflect the underlying distribution. Timestamp Types may affect both the sampling and the evaluation phases, as they may result in a dissimilar distribution. Finally, while the Time Frame of the two datasets is the same, we argue that existing metrics such as Area Under Time (AUT) [ 29 ] capture only a temporal snapshot of performance, and may be the result of a lucky time frame—we call this bias factor Temporal Luck . 
 
 
 In the remainder of this paper, we investigate how the identified factors interplay and may affect realistic evaluations. Furthermore, we provide recommendations on how to prevent them.

 III Experimental Settings 

 
 We first provide a brief overview of the datasets, classifiers, and metrics used in the remainder of the paper. 
 
 
 Datasets 

 
 To investigate the four identified factors, we use both D A D_{A} [ 37 ] and D T D_{T} [ 12 ] (see §II ). Where appropriate, we generalize our findings on the wider Android malware population by utilizing metadata from AndroZoo [ 1 ] , obtaining reports from VirusTotal [ 4 ] and unifying family labels from individual AV engines using Euphony [ 21 ] . Finally, we demonstrate how to practically apply our sampling and evaluation guidelines from §IV by collecting and evaluating a recent dataset from 2021–2023 in §V . More details about the datasets used can be found in the appendix Appendix A . 
 
 
 
 Classifiers 

 
 All our experiments are carried out on five representative Android malware classifiers: 
 
 
 
 • 
 
 Drebin [ 11 ] : Linear SVM using binary features from static analysis (e.g., APIs, URLs, Activities). 
 
 • 
 
 DeepDrebin [ 20 ] : Uses the same features as Drebin , with an MLP classifier. 
 
 • 
 
 MalScan [ 36 ] : Graph-based approach using degree centrality with a Random Forest classifier. 
 
 • 
 
 Ramda [ 23 ] : DNN model combining VAE and MLP, focusing on sensitive APIs, Permissions, and Intents. 
 
 • 
 
 Hcc [ 15 ] : Autoencoder-guided embedding using malware family labels for enhanced class separability. 
 
 
 These classifier represent diverse feature representations and model architectures: token-based ( Drebin , DeepDrebin , Hcc ), API-based ( Ramda ), and graph-based ( MalScan ); and models including linear SVM, MLP, autoencoder, DNN, and Random Forest. This heterogeneity allows for robust, representation-agnostic insights. Notably, Hcc [ 15 ] builds upon Drebin and DeepDrebin and represents the current state-of-the-art. 
 
 
 
 Metrics 

 
 For each factor, we assess their impact using a combination of standard performance metric, the F 1 F_{1} -Score, the AUT metric for temporal evaluation [ 29 ] , and the Family Overlap measure introduced in §II to observe the distribution shift of malware families independent from the feature representation or classifier used (see Appendix C ). 
 
 
 
 
 IV Novel Spatio-Temporal Bias Factors 

 
 Following the observations in §II , we introduce five novel factors for spatio-temporal experimental biases: Timestamp Type ( §IV-A ), Temporal Luck ( §IV-B ), App Markets ( §IV-C ), VirusTotal Threshold ( §IV-D ), and Dataset Size ( §IV-E ). For each factor, we begin with a hypothesis, then evaluate its impact, and finally provide practical recommendations. 
 
 
 IV-A Timestamp Types 

 
 IV-A 1 Hypothesis 

 
 apk often have multiple associated timestamps, such as file creation and market publication dates. These correspond to different points in time in the apk ’s life cycle. For longitudinal evaluation, the choice of timestamps for temporal slicing affects when an apk is thought to have appeared in the real world. We focus on two main types: creation timestamps, which are locally embedded and potentially unreliable, and publication timestamps, which reflect when an app was made available to users. 
 
 
 
 Hypothesis: Timestamp Type 
 
 
 The choice of timestamp type (creation vs. publication) affects the temporal distribution of malicious apk in the sampled dataset, which in turn impacts performance evaluation. In particular, using unreliable timestamps (e.g., creation date) may misrepresent when apk or threats actually appeared.

 IV-A 2 Impact 

 
 We find that creation timestamps are an unreliable proxy for the actual release time, often deviating significantly from publication timestamps. As a result, using creation dates can shift apk outside from their true temporal context, leading to incorrect assumptions on the time a threat emerged. This temporal misalignment can distort sampling and, consequently, affect performance evaluations. 
 
 
 Approximating Creation and Publication Timestamps 

 
 First, we need to decide how to approximate creation and publication timestamps. D T D_{T} [ 12 ] uses the dex_date , i.e., the filesystem timestamp that indicates when the classes.dex file in an apk was last modified. dex_dates have been commonly used to approximate creation dates for time-aware evaluations [ 29 , 6 ] . Instead, D A D_{A} [ 37 ] uses the VT first_submission_date , the timestamp that indicates when an apk was first submitted to vt . Conceptually, VT_first_submission_dates should be considered publication timestamps, as they indicate when a sample was first submitted to vt ( vt ). However, we observed that there are minimal differences between dex_dates and VT_first_submission_dates . As shown in Figure 3 , reordering D A D_{A} and D T D_{T} using the other dataset timestamp type: (that is, D T D_{T} sorted by VT_first_submission_dates and D A D_{A} by dex_dates ) results in the vast majority of the samples lying on the diagonal (or in its proximity). This suggests that samples are generally submitted to VT shortly after their build date. Therefore, we classify VT_first_submission_dates as creation timestamps. As GooglePlay dates became available on AndroZoo after June 2020 [ 5 ] and the time frame of D T D_{T} and D A D_{A} precedes that, we require an approximation of publication timestamps for evaluations prior to 2020. We note that the AndroZoo crawl_dates for GooglePlay represent an exceptionally accurate approximation of official GooglePlay publication dates, with a median difference of only 5 days 1 1

 Timestamps Impact on Sampling 

 
 We now evaluate whether creation timestamps or publication timestamps produce more realistic data distributions. To this end, we perform experiments exclusively on D T D_{T} , since most of the information needed to perform the same evaluations on D A D_{A} is not available. More specifically, AndroZoo’s crawl_dates for D A D_{A} would indicate that most samples are from 2018, and VirusShare, from which D A D_{A} malware is sampled, is no longer accessible due to severe hardware faults (as of May/June 2025). 
 
 
 Since goodware has minimal effect on the drift of D T D_{T} [ 16 ] , we focus on the impact that timestamps have on the distribution of malware samples. We imitated the sampling described in [ 12 ] , which used dex_dates (creation timestamps). We identically and independently (randomly) sample five malware datasets from the full AndroZoo population using the sampling criteria of D T D_{T} : time frame 2014–2018, vtt ( vtt ) ≥ 4 \geq 4 , dex_dates as timestamps, and an identical number of malicious samples per month, accumulating to 26,387 26,387 malicious apk per dataset. We then analyzed their “family overlaps” in Figure 4 (as in §II ), from which we notice a stark difference between 𝒟 v ​ t ​ t ​ 4 2025 \mathcal{D}_{vtt4}^{2025} and D T D_{T} . We hypothesize that this is due to the fact that AndroZoo has changed significantly since D T D_{T} was originally sampled. Specifically, we observe that several malicious apk with creation timestamps older than their publication dates were added to AndroZoo since the release of D T D_{T} . This suggests that malware samples with dex_dates belonging to that period were added long after the dataset was sampled.
To further confirm our reasoning, we contacted the authors of [ 12 ] to find out the date when D T D_{T} was sampled. They informed us that the first three years 2014–2016 correspond to the dataset published with [ 29 ] and were sampled in early 2018, while the last two years 2017–2018 were sampled in early 2019 as part of the dataset released with [ 30 ] .
By analyzing the AndroZoo metadata for the samples in D T D_{T} , we notice that the youngest sample in 2014–2016 was added to AndroZoo in April 2017 and that the youngest in 2017–2018 in January 2019. Given this information, we repeat the previous experiment filtering out all samples with dex_date between 2014–2016 whose crawl_date is subsequent to mid-2017 and all samples with dex_date between 2017–2018 whose crawl_date indicates that the samples were added to AndroZoo after January 2019. In this way, we try to emulate the sampling process of [ 29 ] and [ 30 ] . 
 
 
 Figure 4 compares the average yearly family overlap for the datasets we sampled. It can be seen that the average family overlap for the datasets sampled in 2025 differs from that of the datasets sampled trying to emulate [ 29 , 30 ] , the latter approaching that of D T D_{T} .

This means that creation timestamps make the snapshot of the data distribution dependent on when a dataset was sampled. This implies that creation timestamps are not reliable in determining when an apk is released in the wild and should be avoided.

 Timestamps Impact on Performance 

 
 We now measure the impact of creation and publication timestamps on the performance of the five classifiers we introduced in §III . As aforementioned, we limit the evaluation to D T D_{T} due to missing information for D A D_{A} . 
 
 
 Hence, we evaluate all five classifiers on D T D_{T} , training on 2016 and testing in 2017–2018, using the crawl_date to reorder apk in the dataset. We eliminate 2014–2015 because AndroZoo had just been created in that period; hence, not many samples were added to the repository in that time frame. Specifically, no samples in D T D_{T} were added to AndroZoo in 2015. Figure 5 shows the performance difference when using dex_dates versus crawl_dates . It is evident that all classifiers exhibit more drift throughout 2017 and the first half of 2018 when using crawl_dates . In contrast, the performance remains stable for all classifiers when using dex_dates . 
 
 
 
 
 IV-A 3 Recommendations 

 
 Given the unreliability of creation timestamps, we advocate against using them, in contrast with previous published work [ 29 , 30 , 12 , 37 ] . Instead, we recommend using publication timestamps as these describe exactly when samples started circulating and are therefore more reliable. In particular, one should use the dates indicating when an apk was uploaded to a market. For Google Play, this corresponds to the “last update date” available for each app. This information is also provided and recommended by AndroZoo for apk downloaded from GooglePlay after June 2020 [ 5 ] . 
 
 
 
 Recommendation: Timestamp Type 
 
 
 Datasets must be sampled using publication timestamps to model the temporal distribution. Official market upload dates should be preferred (e.g., GooglePlay publication dates); otherwise, crawl_dates from AndroZoo can be an approximation. 
 
 
 
 
 
 IV-B Temporal Luck 

 
 IV-B 1 Hypothesis 

 
 Two datasets D 1 D_{1} and D 2 D_{2} , sampled from different time frames (e.g., 2012–2014 and 2016–2018), can be expected to represent different distributions (e.g., they may vary in terms of family composition). Therefore, no meaningful conclusions can be drawn from comparing the performance of a malware classifier on D 1 D_{1} with the performance of another malware detection method evaluated on D 2 D_{2} . This issue of comparing malware detection methods on different datasets presenting a distribution mismatch often arises when researchers directly compare their results with those reported in other related papers (“comparisons on paper”). 
 
 
 Given two malware detection approaches A 1 A_{1} and A 2 A_{2} , a straightforward solution would be to evaluate both on the same dataset, as done in recent work (e.g., [ 37 , 36 , 12 ] ). Nevertheless, this may not be sufficient to conclude which method performs best, as the reported results may still be influenced by what we call Temporal Luck . This phenomenon arises when an arbitrary time-aware split into training and testing inflates the performance of a given method, due to the training set, by chance, being “exceptionally” good or the subsequent testing period not drifting significantly. In practical terms, given a dataset 𝒟 2012 − 2020 \mathcal{D}_{2012-2020} , A 1 A_{1} may perform better than A 2 A_{2} when training on 2012 and testing on 2013–2020, whereas A 2 A_{2} may exhibit superior performance when training on 2013 and testing on 2014–2020. Hence, we postulate the following hypothesis. 
 
 
 
 Hypothesis: Temporal Luck 
 
 
 Given a dataset, the performance of a malware detection method may vary based on the temporal train/test splits.

 IV-B 2 Impact 

 
 Figure 6 provides an overview of the impact of Temporal Luck on both D A D_{A} and D T D_{T} . The settings we employ are the following: we train classifiers on data from each year in the time frame 2014–2018 and test them in the years that follow. In practice, we divide the datasets into four subsets: the first having 2014 as training window and testing on 2015–2018, the second having 2015 for training and 2016–2018 for testing, and so on. It is visually evident in D T D_{T} that significantly different performance profiles may be obtained depending on the temporal split. This can also be observed for D A D_{A} , albeit to a lesser extent. 
 
 
 To quantify Temporal Luck in D A D_{A} and D T D_{T} , we compute the AUT ( AUT ) score when training on one year and testing only on the following year (i.e., (i) train on 2014 and test on 2015, (ii) train on 2015 and test on 2016, etc.). The AUT summarizes the performance of a classifier over a certain time frame, and in the presence of Temporal Luck, it can emphasize when a proposed approach is consistently working across different temporal splits. The results are summarized in Table II . We can again observe significantly different results on D T D_{T} for all classifiers and temporal configurations, and smaller differences for D A D_{A} (with a larger difference in the last column). 
 
 
 While revealing substantial performance variation across training years, the AUT may complicate the comparison between different state-of-the-art classifiers, as no individual classifier clearly performs better than the others in all scenarios. For instance, Drebin performs better than DeepDrebin in 2014–2015 and 2016–2017 on D T D_{T} . The reverse is true for the other two temporal configurations. Hcc , the current state-of-the-art in Android malware detection, shows the highest AUT in 2016–2017 and 2017–2018 on D A D_{A} only. Given these mixed results, it is legitimate to wonder which one should be considered the superior classifier.

 IV-C App Markets 

 
 IV-C 1 Hypothesis 

 
 Android apps are distributed across multiple markets, each serving different regions and user bases. For example, GooglePlay is the dominant platform in Western regions, while alternative markets are more prevalent in Eastern contexts. When constructing malware datasets, researchers often sample from a single market—typically GooglePlay—especially when focusing on region-specific threats or working within the constraints of public data (see §V ). While this yields datasets representative of their target market, it may limit generalizability. In contrast, recent studies [ 29 , 12 , 37 ] adopt multi-market sampling to increase diversity and scale. However, such strategies may unintentionally introduce shortcut learning, as classifiers could learn market-correlated features instead of security-relevant ones [ 10 ] . 
 
 
 Table IV shows the market composition of the full AndroZoo, D A D_{A} and D T D_{T} datasets, based on metadata from AndroZoo [ 1 ] . The data reveal substantial imbalance: certain markets contribute disproportionately to the AndroZoo population, and the D A D_{A} and D T D_{T} datasets differ distinctly in their sampling. In particular, D A D_{A} exhibits a pronounced sampling bias, sourcing malware primarily from VirusShare and goodware from GooglePlay. 
 
 
 We therefore hypothesize that an imbalanced market composition in training data, where malware and goodware are predominantly sampled from different markets, results in statistically significant-degradation of classifier performance when evaluated on market-balanced or differently-composed test sets. This is consistent with previous findings on shortcut learning [ 10 ] and is testable using market metadata in AndroZoo. 
 
 
 
 Hypothesis: App Markets 
 
 
 Altering the composition of App Markets used in dataset sampling significantly affects goodware and malware class-distribution, and leads to reduced detection performance (e.g., F 1 F_{1} -Score or AUT) when models are evaluated on out-of-sample market distributions.

 Similarity of apk 

 
 We begin by analyzing the impact on performance when training and testing are conducted on the same markets and on different markets. As shown in Table VI , training and testing on the same market consistently yields higher F 1 F_{1} -Score scores compared to cross-market scenarios. For example, Drebin achieves an increase of 0.551 and 0.361 in F 1 F_{1} -Score score when evaluated on the same market. These results indicate notable differences in the characteristics of goodware and malware across market groups. 
 
 
 This point is further underscored when examining the F 1 F_{1} -Score of classifiers trained on samples from one source, but tested on 𝒟 E ​ V ​ E ​ N \mathcal{D}_{EVEN} (samples from GP and 3PM). For example, training on 𝒟 G ​ P \mathcal{D}_{GP} and testing on 𝒟 E ​ V ​ E ​ N \mathcal{D}_{EVEN} yields F 1 F_{1} -Score of 0.395 and 0.511 for DeepDrebin and MalScan , respectively—significantly lower than the scores observed when testing on 𝒟 G ​ P \mathcal{D}_{GP} alone. Similar trends are observed for 𝒟 3 ​ P ​ M \mathcal{D}_{3PM} and are consistent for all classifiers. 
 
 
 
 Combining markets for training/testing 

 
 Prior work [ 29 , 12 , 37 ] has adopted multi-market sampling. Here we analyze training on mixed markets and testing on mixed or single markets. We find that, regardless of the proportion of Google Play to 3PM apps (e.g., 1:1 or 4:1), the F 1 F_{1} -Score lies within the range of testing on single markets. 
 
 
 
 Goodware and Malware from different markets 

 
 Dataset compositions that include goodware and malware from distinct markets lead to unrealistically-high F 1 F_{1} -Score. This aligns with findings from [ 10 ] , where classifiers inadvertently learn spurious correlations, distinguishing app origins rather than actual differences between goodware and malware. These results clearly demonstrate the influence of app markets on classifier performance and the risk of introducing unintentional biases. 
 
 
 
 Takeaways 

 
 Arp et al. [ 10 ] showed that sampling malware and goodware from different markets can lead to spurious correlations. We extend this finding by showing apps from different markets are inherently different (even if class ratio stays consistent) and training on one market does not transfer well to another. Finally, we observed that testing on mixed markets may give unclear performance that skews towards the majority market. 
 
 
 
 
 IV-C 3 Recommendations 

 
 It is imperative that the sampling is not biased such that goodware and malware originate from different markets. To ensure this, one should sample goodware and malware from the same market. If you wish to use multiple markets, you must sample from each separately, and test only on single markets to obtain the best and worst case. 
 
 
 
 Recommendation: App Markets 
 
 
 The App Markets distribution must be consistent between goodware and malware apps, to avoid spatial bias. When performing multi-market evaluation, you must sample from each market separately, and report the single-market test performance to obtain the best and worst case. 
 
 
 
 
 
 IV-D VirusTotal Threshold 

 
 IV-D 1 Hypothesis 

 
 Although vtt is generally considered by the community as a labeling tool, in pratice it also acts as a sampling parameter. For example, it excludes all samples that have a number of detections between 1 and the chosen vtt value from the base population (these samples are usually called greyware ). For this reason, we expect vtt to play a role in the distribution of malware samples within a dataset. This is particularly evident in AndroZoo, for which we posit that selecting different vtt values significantly affects the population from which to sample, leading to different malware distributions. 
 
 
 
 Hypothesis: VirusTotal Threshold 
 
 
 The choice of vtt affects the distribution from which Android malware is sampled, because it acts as a sampling parameter which filters the original population. 
 
 
 
 
 IV-D 2 Impact 

 
 Starting from our preliminary observation in §II , we investigate the impact of vtt on malware distribution. To this end, we use the two sampling configurations described in §IV-A2 : (i) we sample in 2025 using D T D_{T} sampling criteria; (ii) we emulate the sampling of [ 29 ] and [ 30 ] by sampling in mid-2017 and 2019 for 2014–2016 and 2017–2018, respectively. We repeat both configurations with vtt =4 and vtt =15. 
 
 
 Figure 8 shows the family overlaps we obtained for all 4 configurations. Interestingly, we notice there is minimal difference between vtt =4 and vtt =15, when you compare orange and blue bars. This suggest that vtt does not directly influence the distribution of a sampled dataset.
However, when observing the distribution of malware in different app markets, we notice that vtt does have an impact. Figure 9 shows the percentage of samples from each market when using a different vtt . By picking a higher vtt , one would inadvertently sample more malware from one specific market. In §IV-C , we showed that this could lead to spurious correlations and one should ensure goodware and malware are sampled from the same market. Furthermore, we observe that using a higher vtt will not only only affect distribution of markets but also reduce the population of available malware (e.g., vtt =4 captures 44% of all malware in AndroZoo, whereas vtt =15 captures 14.11%).

 IV-D 3 Recommendations 

 
 We note that vtt does not directly affect the family overlap of malicious samples. However, it does affect the markets the malware is sampled from and, consequently, the performance (see §IV-C ) and the amount of malware available in the wild. Therefore, the lower the vtt , the more representative the sampled dataset would be. Given the finding of [ 27 ] , namely that a minimum of 4 detections is required to reduce noisy labels, we recommend to use vtt =4. 
 
 
 
 Recommendation: VirusTotal Threshold 
 
 
 You must use low VTT (e.g., VTT =4 [ 27 ] ) to sample from a distribution of app markets closer to the original population. Using high VTT will limit the distribution of malware apk . 
 
 
 
 Nevertheless, dealing with grayware remains an open problem that needs addressing. We further discuss this in §VI . 
 
 
 
 
 IV-E Dataset Size 

 
 IV-E 1 Hypothesis 

 
 Ideally, we would evaluate the performance of our Android classifiers against the entire Android population across all of time (or some valid surrogate, such as AndroZoo). However, AndroZoo alone contains over 25 million samples (at the time of writing) spread across 10+ years, which makes any performance evaluation on the full population infeasible. At the same time, arbitrarily choosing a “small” sample size may produce datasets not representative of the underlying population, even if a smaller time frame was selected. 
 
 
 Many influential papers in Android malware detection, such as [ 37 , 12 , 29 , 15 ] , lack a clear rationale on the size of their dataset. Instead, some arbitrarily large sample size is chosen (most likely due to computational limitations) and domain-specific constraints such as Tesseract’s Temporal training consistency (C1), Temporal gw/mw windows consistency (C2) and Realistic malware-to-goodware ratio in testing (C3) [ 29 ] are enforced. On the other hand, Miranda et al. [ 28 ] proposed a statistical method for determining the minimum sample size for Android malware datasets, but do not enforce C2 or C3. Rather, opting to rely fully on statistical means to determine a representative dataset. As previously shown in Figure 1 , even following all prior guidelines and enforcing [ 29 ] does not completely eliminate spatial and temporal bias. As such, we hypothesize that solely relying on a statistically guided sampling methodology without considering domain knowledge-guided constraints will be insufficient in eliminating spatial-temporal bias. We note that C1 is generally reliant on the Timestamps choice and, as long as proper temporal evaluation is performed, is implicitly applied. 
 
 
 
 Hypothesis: Dataset Size 
 
 
 Purely-statistical methods to determine Dataset Size under represent the distribution of the Android population and introduce spatial and temporal bias in the dataset. 
 
 
 
 
 IV-E 2 Impact 

 
 We begin exploring a statistically guided sampling methodology for Android malware detection. Based on the traditional Margin of Error (MoE) formula [ 13 ] using the finite population correction, Miranda et al. [ 28 ] proposed DaDa , an approach that computes the smallest sample size for a dataset to be representative of a given population. They use the Bonferroni correction to account for general Android characteristics, such as three intervals of apk Size, 10 common boolean Android permissions extracted with Droidlysis [ 9 ] , and year of release ranging from 2011 to 2018. However, there is no clear evidence that apk size or common boolean Android permissions are important aspects in Android Malware classification. In fact, we noticed that apk Size increases across time, questioning if this is an appropriate characteristic for sampling (cf. Figure 12 in the Appendix). 
 
 
 Improving DaDa 

 
 Instead of applying the Bonferroni Correction , we propose focusing on the manual enforcement of the temporal (C2) and spatial (C3) constraints introduced in [ 29 ] . These two characteristics are not only supported by prior work but are also directly measurable via metadata available from AndroZoo and controllable during the sampling process. While the Bonferroni correction enhances statistical validity by reducing the risk of false positives, its use in combination with numerous characteristics — such as apk size and a set of common Android permissions — under the constraints of C2 and C3 would demand an impractically large sample size (see Figure 13 in the Appendix). Although larger samples generally yield more representative results, there is a clear trade-off: applying strict statistical corrections to characteristics with limited demonstrated impact on malware dataset evaluation can lead to diminishing returns. In this context, it is more pragmatic to prioritize a smaller number of well-validated factors over attempting to control for many uncertain ones. 
 
 
 We use the same confidence level of C = 99 % C=99\% and margin of error δ = 0.015 \delta=0.015 as Miranda et al. [ 28 ] . We enforce a 9:1 goodware-to-malware ratio such that it satisfies the spatial constraint C3 of Tesseract [ 29 ] . We then enforce the temporal constraint C2 of Tesseract [ 29 ] by sampling either monthly/yearly based on only the MoE formula. 
 
 
 We compare the different configurations of MoE against DaDa across the same timeframe as D A D_{A} and D T D_{T} , imitating the configurations both datasets used for sampling. We used dex_dates as they were easily accessible unlike VT_first_submission_dates . We were unable to use crawl_date despite our recommendation in §IV-A as AndroZoo was not properly established and crawling was inconsistent in 2015. It is evident that due to the relatively small sample size of DaDa , the amount of malware is too little for proper evaluation. The same can be said with MoE/Year and MoE/Spatial/Year where the average malware per year is 122 and 61, respectively.

 Recommendation: Dataset Size

 V Piecing It All Together: A New Dataset 

 
 Following from our recommendations in §IV , we now show how to apply our guidelines in practice by creating a new dataset and evaluating the performance of the Android malware detectors on it. 
 
 
 V-A Composing the Dataset 

 
 As a first step, we sample a dataset considering the five factors introduced in the previous sections. 
 
 
 Time frame selection 

 
 Guided by prior work showing that malware labels tend to stabilize within one year [ 27 , 38 ] , we sample three years of data spanning from 2021 to 2023. This period is recent enough to reflect the current Android malware landscape, while also being long enough to capture the temporal dynamics in Android apps. 
 
 
 
 Market selection 

 
 To eliminate the bias caused by sampling from different markets as discussed in §IV-C , we solely sample apps from GooglePlay for this case study. 
 
 
 
 Timestamps 

 
 We use publication dates to accurately represent when an app appears in the wild. As discussed §IV-A , this is the most reliable timestamp information and in line with the recommendations of the AndroZoo authors (see Alecci et al. [ 5 ] ). 
 
 
 
 VTT selection 

 
 In §IV-D , we advocate for the use of lower VirusTotal thresholds. Therefore, we set the VTT to 4, following the recommendations in Pendlebury et al. [ 29 ] . This threshold will be used for selecting samples based on the publicly available AndroZoo metadata. 
 
 
 
 Sampling size 

 
 We apply MoE/Spatial/Month, our improved version of DaDa (introduced in §IV-E ), which ensures a statistically representative selection of both malware and goodware samples. In accordance with C3 constraints [ 29 ] , it enforces a distribution of 10 % 10\% malware and 90 % 90\% goodware.  VI Discussion 

 
 While our paper extends the set of best practices for evaluating Android malware classifiers under spatio-temporal drift proposed in prior work [ 10 , 29 , 31 , 32 , 18 ] , there are still several open challenges the community faces. In the following, we contextualize our findings by discussing related challenges. 
 
 
 Markets in AndroZoo 

 
 AndroZoo [ 1 ] contains primarily apk from GooglePlay and other western markets, under-representing other regions where Android is the most popular operating system, especially China [ 2 ] . It has been noted that Chinese app markets are generally less active in protecting mobile users from deceptive or abusive actors; therefore, they tend to have a higher prevalence of malware [ 35 ] . At the same time, AndroZoo is currently the best repository we have as a community for benign and malicious apk . 
 
 
 
 Grayware 

 
 Our work provides evidence that lower VTT values better represent the malware population. However, how to account for grayware , i.e., apk detected by at least one AV but less than VTT, which are predominant in AndroZoo, remains an open problem. Works such as [ 8 ] have attempted to categorize different types of grayware in GooglePlay. However, the taxonomy of grayware remains a problem for Android malware detection. 
 
 
 
 Single point of failure 

 
 A major unspoken risk comes from the fact that AndroZoo and VirusTotal are single points of failure for the malware community. Alecci et al. [ 5 ] highlight that AndroZoo in 2024 reached 448TB of data and will continue to grow. This may eventually become unsustainable for a single research team. On the other hand, VirusTotal puts a cap of 500 queries per user per day, significantly hindering the curation of new datasets. Considering the instability of labels in the wild [ 27 ] , simply relying on AndroZoo is not sufficient. As a community, we should try to reason about what we could do to build more resilient repositories. 
 
 
 
 Evolution of C3 [ 29 ] 

 
 We observed that for AndroZoo in 2014–2018, malware is not consistently 10% across all months. However, obtaining a “true” ratio would require help from multiple industrial partners. [ 29 ] estimated the percentage of malware samples at 10 % 10\% by averaging various sources in 2019, which may no longer be valid in the current landscape. For lack of a better source, using 10 % 10\% as a spatial constraint will allow consistency in sampled datasets. Future works should investigate the temporal evolution of realistic malware ratios. 
 
 
 
 K-Fold evaluation 

 
 Evaluating an ML-based malware detector without temporal constraints would require the use of K-fold cross-validation as shown in [ 29 ] . While our study is focused on scenarios under temporal drift, the proposed spatial guidelines are still relevant for non-temporal evaluations. This includes Timestamp Type §IV-A , App Markets §IV-C , VirusTotal Threshold §IV-D , and Dataset Size §IV-E . 
 
 
 
 Windows malware 

 
 While our study is focused on Android malware, many of the challenges can be shared with the Windows domain. For example, getting a reliable timestamp source can be difficult, especially when there is no active crawler like AndroZoo. Instead, [ 22 ] recommends using first seen as the best estimate for Windows PE. Another common challenge is Temporal Luck, since both domains are evaluated in a temporal manner. Thus, our recommendations for avoiding “Lucky splits” could be beneficial in the Windows domain. 
 
 
 
 
 VII Related Work 

 
 In this section, we cover the research most closely related to our work. We present both seminal research papers tackling the wider problem of achieving fair evaluations in ML -based computer security research and more directly comparable publications on dataset sampling and debiasing. 
 
 
 Guidelines for realistic ML for computer security 

 
 Our research aligns with efforts to establish guidelines for realistic evaluations in security-related applications. Sommer and Paxson [ 32 ] highlighted key challenges in applying ML to network intrusion detection systems (NIDS), pointing out fundamental issues that persist in this domain. Rossow et al. [ 31 ] and Van der Kouwe et al. [ 34 ] further analyzed common shortcomings and best practices in security system evaluations. However, these works primarily identify challenges and methodological pitfalls without quantifying their impact on classifier performance. Arp et al. [ 10 ] extended this line of work by demonstrating the effects of these pitfalls in real-world scenarios, although their focus remained on general cybersecurity. 
 
 
 In contrast, our work investigates the specific properties and risk factors during dataset creation for the Android malware domain that lead to many of the pitfalls identified in prior studies. 
 
 
 
 Temporal evaluations 

 
 Other relevant work includes the domain of temporal evaluation primarily raised by Allix et al [ 6 ] and Miller et al. [ 27 ] . Recently, Pendlebury et al. [ 29 ] proposed Tesseract as a framework to measure impact of performance decay. We expand on Tesseract by proposing a new evaluation guideline in Temporal Luck. Additionally, we address the validity of creation timestamps by experimentally showing the impact of timestamp inconsistencies and providing practical recommendations for researchers to follow ( §IV-A ). 
 
 
 
 Realistic datasets for ML-based cybersecurity 

 
 Prior work also focused on the problem of realistic datasets and benchmark datasets for ML-based cybersecurity. Sommer and Paxon [ 32 ] highlighted the challenges of creating realistic network intrusion detection datasets, mostly due to privacy concerns; attempts to create datasets artificially, such as DARPA98-99 [ 24 ] and KDD99 [ 3 ] , failed because they introduced artifacts [ 25 , 17 , 26 ] . Similarly, Jian et al. [ 22 ] created a benchmark dataset for Windows malware by sampling from VirusShare, following a similar dataset curation pipeline to the one we presented. However, we focus on factors related to representativeness, such as sample size and markets, rather than the quality of family labels. [ 14 ] also presented a preliminary discussion on dataset size and markets but lacks empirical evaluation or concrete recommendations, while we provide both. Notably, [ 14 ] calls for better dataset practice—our work directly answers its call. Recently, Flood et al. [ 18 ] identified six poor practices in NIDS-specific dataset including poor data diversity, highly dependent features, unclear ground truths, traffic collapse artificial diversity, and wrong labels. Instead of focusing on the limitations of network datasets, we focus our attention on the Android malware domain, where the presence of the AndroZoo [ 1 , 7 , 5 ] has offered more opportunities to build more realistic datasets. Furthermore, all our factors are directly applicable during the sampling process, helping to prevent the identified “bad” choices in [ 18 ] . [ 19 ] analyzed three datasets factors (i.e. class imbalance, quality, and timelines) by examining three state-of-the-art datasets. We differ from this work as we not only show how our sampling factors can affect the sampled distribution but also give actionable recommendations that researchers can follow to reduce the biases highlighted in [ 19 ] . Additionally, we do not limit our work to only two state-of-the-art datasets, but instead include the wider Android population in our experimental analysis. In conclusion, we identify five factors that can affect realistic evaluations and that can be controlled with actionable recommendations ( §IV ). 
 
 
 
 Research on Android malware sampling 

 
 Prior work has also attempted to provide algorithms to create representative Android benchmark datasets. Miranda et al. proposed DaDa [ 28 ] , a two step sampling approach for debiasing Android malware datasets, which combines statistical methods with general apk characteristics. The first step is to randomly sample a representative dataset from a population using the margin of classical error to then debias it. However, in §IV-E , we have shown that DaDa lacks important domain-specific knowledge, which is crucial at the sampling stage. In particular, it violates C ​ 3 C3 of [ 29 ] and P1 of [ 10 ] . Recently, Thirumuruganathan et al. [ 33 ] proposed an approach to detect and mitigate sampling bias between a labeled training dataset and unlabeled testing dataset based on domain discrimination, whereas our work focuses on the broader picture, questioning how well datasets represent the real-world population, rather than finding a classifier that fits both datasets. 
 
 
 
 
 VIII Conclusion 

 
 We have demonstrated that current best practices [ 10 , 29 , 31 , 32 , 18 ] for dataset curation and evaluation of Android malware datasets are insufficient to eliminate spatial and temporal biases. 
 
 
 To better understand and mitigate this phenomenon, we have identified five factors —i.e., Timestamp Type, Temporal Luck, App Markets, VirusTotal Threshold, and Dataset Size—that can directly or indirectly affect the performance evaluation of a learning-based classifier. We have provided actionable recommendations to mitigate their impact through proper sampling strategies and evaluation guidelines. 
 
 
 While the problem of sampling bias remains challenging and vast, this paper provides a first solid milestone towards controllable factors and actionable recommendations, which will allow for more realistic evaluations and fairer comparisons of learning-based systems for malware detection.

 Appendix C D: Family overlap 

 
 Family Overlap Metric 

 
 In our analysis, we are interested in describing the temporal distribution shift of the malware population within one dataset without having to rely on any classifier’s performance. Therefore, we introduce family overlap ( Φ \Phi ), a metric that models the shift in malware distribution within a dataset. Shown previously in Figure 2 , it allows us to obtain more general insights into the dataset, without relying on the representation or the classifier employed. From this, we can observe how certain sampling parameters or algorithms influence the resulting dataset composition.
While methods quantifying the difference of distributions exist (e.g., the Kullback-Leibler divergence), they typically require a representation space to operate in. In contrast, we designed family overlap to be representation-independent and interpretable, and specifically designed based on domain knowledge from the malware domain.
The family overlap ( Φ \Phi ) measures the the percentage of samples in a dataset 𝒟 \mathcal{D} belonging to a family that was already known in different dataset 𝒟 ref \mathcal{D}_{\text{ref}} .
